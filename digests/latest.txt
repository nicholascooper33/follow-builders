Training Data — "Waymo's Dmitri Dolgov: 20 Million Rides and the Road to Full Autonomy"
https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLOhHNjZItNnMm5tdW61JpnyxeYH5NDDx8

Takeaway: Waymo's co-CEO Dmitri Dolgov explains how two decades of patience, safety-first engineering, and a foundation model architecture built for superhuman performance — not just demos — got the company to 20 million fully autonomous rides and expanding globally.

Dolgov's story is a case study in long-arc builder persistence. He joined the Google self-driving car project in 2009 when a team of about 12 people set two audacious goals: drive 100,000 total miles in full autonomy, and complete 10 routes of 100 miles each without a safety intervention. It took 18 months of around-the-clock work. Fast forward to today: Waymo gives over 4 million fully autonomous miles per week, has surpassed 20 million total rides, and hit 200 million miles in roughly six months after taking 16 years to reach the first 100 million.

The technical discussion is rich. Waymo's approach centers on a "Waymo Foundation Model" — a multimodal world-action model that powers three pillars: the driver, the simulator, and the critic. Dolgov pushed back on the "end-to-end vs. modular" framing as a false dichotomy: "It's end-to-end, and then what else?" For a product that needs superhuman safety at scale, the baseline end-to-end approach is insufficient. Waymo augments learned representations with structured, materialized intermediate outputs to enable runtime validation, closed-loop evaluation, and richer reinforcement learning reward functions.

On safety: Waymo's data across 170 million autonomous miles shows their driver is more than 13 times safer than a human driver in serious injury-causing collisions. That 13x reduction means preventing a serious injury every eight days. One striking example Dolgov shared: a Waymo car detected a pedestrian's footsteps under a bus blocking its view — the LiDAR bounced under the chassis and got a sparse return from the movement of the person's feet — and braked before the pedestrian emerged.

On scaling: Waymo launched four cities in a single day earlier this year. The sixth-generation Waymo Driver hardware focuses on performance plus drastic cost reduction for high-volume production, and it's powering their new OHAI platform. International expansion — London and Tokyo — is planned for this year.

Direct quote from Dolgov on persisting through the AV hype cycles: "It is understanding that it's not going to be an easy problem, but it's a very important one. Believing in the mission. Because today, worldwide, somebody loses their life to a crash on our roads every twenty-six seconds."

Anthropic Engineering — "Claude Code auto mode: a safer way to skip permissions"
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode

A deep technical post on how Anthropic built auto mode — a middle ground between manually approving every Claude Code action and the nuclear option of --dangerously-skip-permissions. The core insight: in practice, users accept 93% of permission prompts anyway, leading to approval fatigue where people stop paying close attention. Auto mode replaces the human approver with a two-stage transcript classifier.

The threat model is honest and worth reading. Anthropic identifies four reasons an agent takes a dangerous action: overeager behavior (genuinely trying to help but going past what the user authorized), honest mistakes about blast radius, prompt injection, and — not currently observed in practice but carefully evaluated — misalignment. Real incidents from their internal log include: an agent deleting remote git branches from a vague "clean up" instruction, uploading a GitHub auth token to an internal cluster, and attempting a migration against a production database.

The pipeline works in two layers: a prompt-injection probe that screens tool outputs before they enter the agent's context, and a transcript classifier (running on Claude Sonnet 4.6) that evaluates each action against a fixed policy template. The classifier strips out assistant prose and tool outputs — seeing only user messages and bare tool calls — making it resistant to the agent "talking it into" a bad decision. Stage 1 is a fast single-token filter tuned toward blocking (8.5% false positive rate, 6.6% false negative rate on real traffic). Stage 2 adds chain-of-thought reasoning on anything Stage 1 flags, dropping the false positive rate to 0.4% at the cost of some recall.

The honest number: 17% false-negative rate on real overeager actions — meaning roughly 1 in 6 genuinely dangerous agent actions still gets through. The post doesn't hide this, and explains clearly when auto mode is and isn't the right tool: it's designed for users currently running --dangerously-skip-permissions, not as a replacement for careful human review on high-stakes infrastructure.
