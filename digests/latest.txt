OpenAI's Dan Roberts: Why AI Can Now Make Discoveries — MAD Podcast with Matt Turck
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWOz2htozfI

AI is no longer just executing tasks we hand it — it is now autonomously making deep scientific discoveries, and this episode is the best explanation yet of how that became possible.

Dan Roberts leads the Foundations of Reinforcement Learning team at OpenAI, and he comes to the role from an unusual path: a PhD in theoretical physics from MIT, work on quantum gravity and black holes, and a postdoc at the Institute for Advanced Study — all before pivoting to AI research around 2016 when AlphaGo caught his attention. That cross-disciplinary background shapes how he thinks about RL: not just as an engineering problem, but as something that can be studied and understood the same way physicists study the universe.

The episode opens with recent news that OpenAI, DeepMind, and Anthropic cracked some of the most famous long-unsolved problems in mathematics — the Erdős problems — a moment host Matt Turck describes as a stunning breakthrough. Dan unpacks what made this possible: reinforcement learning, which he calls the most important paradigm in AI right now. He explains how RL differs from pretraining, what scaling laws look like for RL versus pretraining, and what this kind of training actually teaches — and doesn't teach — a model.

A key thread through the conversation is the value of contrarian conviction. Dan notes that one of ChatGPT's breakthroughs required the team to "assume it was false" — to go against the grain with strong conviction and follow a long calculation path to its conclusion. That same disposition, he argues, is what allows reasoning models to work through hard problems rather than defaulting to the most likely next token.

The closing note is the most forward-looking: "I feel really excited that we will get to really answer a lot of fundamental questions in the field of science that we care about with the aid — or the models being the driving force." Dan's framing is that we are moving from AI as a tool scientists use to AI as a co-discoverer, and that this transition is already underway.

If you want to understand why AI's leap into autonomous scientific discovery happened now, this is the episode to listen to.

New in Claude Managed Agents: Dreaming, Outcomes, and Multiagent Orchestration — Anthropic
https://claude.com/blog/new-in-claude-managed-agents

Anthropic shipped three significant upgrades to Claude Managed Agents that collectively push toward agents capable of self-improvement, self-evaluation, and parallel execution.

Dreaming is the headline feature: a scheduled process that reviews past agent sessions, extracts patterns, and curates memories so agents improve over time — without requiring a human to review every session. Teams can configure how much control they want, from fully automatic memory updates to human-in-the-loop review. Harvey (legal AI) saw ~6x completion rate improvement in tests using dreaming.

Outcomes lets developers write a rubric for what success looks like. A separate grader evaluates agent output against that rubric in its own context window — isolated from the agent's reasoning — and sends the agent back for another pass if the bar isn't met. In Anthropic's internal benchmarks, outcomes improved task success by up to 10 points, with the largest gains on the hardest tasks. File generation also improved: +8.4% on docx, +10.1% on pptx.

Multiagent orchestration allows a lead agent to break complex jobs into pieces and delegate each to specialist subagents with their own model, prompt, and tools — running in parallel on a shared filesystem. Netflix's platform team is using this to analyze logs from hundreds of builds simultaneously, surfacing only the patterns worth acting on. Spiral by Every uses a Haiku lead agent to delegate drafting to Opus subagents, with outcomes enforcing editorial quality on every draft before it's returned.
