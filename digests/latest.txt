AI & I by Every — "How a Writer Uses AI Without Losing His Voice"

One-sentence takeaway: A working writer and builder explains how he uses AI aggressively to build personal software while protecting the slow, deep attention that makes his creative work actually his.

Greg doesn't touch the internet or his phone until long after lunch each day — the moment he picks up his phone, he says, he "can't go into any kind of deep thinking place or deep attention place or deep focus place." He keeps a dedicated laptop for writing that blocks everything else. These aren't affectations: they're the structural conditions that make serious creative work possible alongside AI tools that are, as host Dan Shipper puts it, "sort of a slot machine."

But Greg is genuinely deep into building with AI. He rebuilt Quicken for himself, replaced Campaign Monitor — where he was spending $67,000 a year on email delivery — with his own Amazon SES-based setup for roughly $150 a year, and built "The Good Place," a private social network just for his paying members where posts disappear in a week, users can only post twice a day, and there's no algorithm. The first thing he built with Claude Code when it launched; now being expanded. He spent about $1,200 a year on Claude access to build all of this: "That is the deal of the century in terms of what you can build."

One of the sharpest lines in the conversation: "If you're not touching it, if you're not using it, if you're not building with it, you can't really comment on it." Greg is skeptical of people who dismiss AI tools without engaging. At the same time, he's clear-eyed about what the tools can't replace — the weird, slow, deeply personal work that only a particular human would think to do. "There aren't that many people who are going to think about or write the weird books that I feel like I'm drawn to write. And as a human, that feels like a valuable thing for me to put my effort in."

The episode is a useful counterweight to both the AI maximalist and AI skeptic positions: a technically engaged creative practitioner who builds seriously with the tools while protecting the human signal those tools are supposed to amplify.

Direct quote: "I wake up and I don't touch the Internet. I won't look at my phone. I won't go online until long after lunch. As soon as I touch my phone, I feel the chemical shift and I can't go into any kind of deep thinking place or deep attention place or deep focus place."

Anthropic Engineering — "An update on recent Claude Code quality reports"
Anthropic traced widely-reported quality regressions to three separate changes: (1) default reasoning effort was dropped from high to medium to reduce UI-freezing latency — the wrong tradeoff, reverted after user feedback; (2) a bug caused Claude to clear its thinking context every turn instead of just once after an hour-long idle, making it seem forgetful; (3) a reasoning effort cap was mistakenly applied to Claude Cowork, limiting depth there too. All three issues were resolved by April 20 (v2.1.116). The postmortem is candid and specific about what failed and what changes will prevent similar regressions.
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem

Anthropic Engineering — "Scaling Managed Agents: Decoupling the brain from the hands"
The central insight: harnesses encode assumptions about what Claude can't do on its own — and those assumptions go stale as models improve. Anthropic built Managed Agents as a hosted service that separates the agent loop (orchestration, context management, error recovery) from tool execution, using OS-style abstractions meant to outlast any particular implementation. The analogy to operating system design is apt: just as read() works the same whether it's hitting a 1970s disk pack or a modern SSD, the agent interface should remain stable as the underlying model and infrastructure evolve.
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/managed-agents

Claude Blog — "New in Claude Managed Agents: self-hosted sandboxes and MCP tunnels"
Claude Managed Agents can now execute tools in a sandbox you control, with your network policies, audit logging, and security tooling already in place. The agent orchestration loop stays on Anthropic's infrastructure; tool execution moves to your perimeter — whether that's your own infrastructure or managed providers like Cloudflare, Daytona, Modal, or Vercel. MCP tunnels let agents connect to private MCP servers inside your enterprise without exposing them to the public internet. Self-hosted sandboxes are in public beta; MCP tunnels are in research preview.
https://claude.com/blog/claude-managed-agents-updates
