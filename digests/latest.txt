No Priors: Building an Autonomous Enterprise for Real-World Services with Netic Founder Melisa Tokmak

Takeaway: Netic is betting that the last mile of AI deployment — deeply operational, industry-specific, human-facing — is where the real competitive moats will be built, and that the big labs are structurally unable to go there.

Melisa Tokmak, founder and CEO of Netic, joined No Priors to talk about what it actually takes to build AI for the "real world" — not the Silicon Valley definition of real world (coding agents, chatbots), but the billion-dollar essential services economy: HVAC companies, plumbing, pet care, roofing, wellness clubs, and hospitality.

Netic sits between large enterprises and their customers, handling every inbound and outbound customer interaction with AI agents — voice calls, texts, web scheduling. Over 70% of their customers are now "Netic first," meaning every initial customer interaction goes through a Netic agent. The company claims to have generated over $600 million in AI-handled revenue for their clients so far.

What makes the episode compelling is Tokmak's clarity on why the big labs won't eat her lunch. It's not that they can't build the features — it's that they don't have the vertical focus, the customer obsession, or the willingness to do the operational heavy lifting these industries require. "OpenAI builds amazing products really fast, but also kills them really fast," she says. Enterprises in essential services need long-term reliability, not pivots.

She also pushes back on the AI rollup model — buying service businesses and optimizing them with AI. Her argument: a rollup's product only works for the companies it bought. Netic's product can work for any real-world business. The compounding network effect is in the platform, not the portfolio.

On hiring, Tokmak has a framework worth noting: she screens for sustained agency over time, not a single impressive moment. "If you have agency, you've shown agency continuously in your life." She asks candidates what the hardest thing they've ever done is — and then digs in, not looking for a career answer, but a life answer.

Her vision of AI's impact that excites her most: education. She grew up in a small town in Turkey with no computer, got a full scholarship to Stanford by finding one or two graduates to review her essays on Facebook Messenger. "No one will be limited anymore. Everything you want to learn and do is kind of in your pocket."

Direct quote: "I think being a founder is a very difficult thing that you have to dedicate your whole life for decades. It is not something to be taken lightly, but people are worried about this too much because I think they're looking at it on like, 'Oh, will I be able to exit in a short timeframe?'"

Anthropic Engineering: How we contain Claude across products

A detailed technical retrospective from Anthropic's engineering team on the hardest security problem in agentic AI: not what the agent does, but what it's able to do. The post covers three isolation patterns across three products (claude.ai, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork) and is unusually candid about what went wrong.

The key framing: there are two ways to cap blast radius — supervise the agent's behavior (human-in-the-loop) or contain the agent's environment (sandboxes, VMs, egress controls). The post argues that behavioral supervision alone is insufficient, because users develop approval fatigue (93% of Claude Code permission prompts were approved) and because model-layer defenses are probabilistic, not deterministic.

Three failures they document openly:
1. A pre-trust-dialog vulnerability in Claude Code, where a malicious .claude/settings.json in a cloned repo could execute hooks before the user had approved the project.
2. A red-team phish in which an employee was tricked into running a prompt that exfiltrated AWS credentials 24 out of 25 times — model-layer defenses couldn't catch it because the user was the injection vector.
3. An egress bypass in Claude Cowork, where a malicious file used the api.anthropic.com allowlist to upload workspace files to an attacker-controlled Anthropic account. The allowlist was designed as a destination filter — it needed to be a capability grant.

The recurring lesson: custom components fail. The mature primitives (gVisor, hypervisors, seccomp, bubblewrap) held. The team's own proxy and allowlist logic are where the incidents landed.

Looking ahead, they flag persistent memory poisoning (CLAUDE.md files and mounted workspaces surviving across sessions become long-lived injection targets) and multi-agent trust escalation (if sub-agent output is treated as higher-trust than raw tool results, you've introduced a new prompt injection vector).

Worth reading in full if you're building any kind of agentic product.
