Unsupervised Learning, Ep 90: AI Pioneer Jürgen Schmidhuber on the State of AI Today
Takeaway: Schmidhuber — often called the "father of modern AI" for his foundational work on LSTMs and meta-learning — is bullish on AI technology but deeply skeptical of the business models of the companies building it, and far less worried about AI safety than most of his peers.

Jacob Efron (Redpoint) sits down with Jürgen Schmidhuber for a wide-ranging conversation that cuts against much of the current hype. Schmidhuber's core thesis: the current CapEx boom in data centers is massively overdone. With compute getting roughly 10x cheaper every five years, companies investing hundreds of billions in GPUs today will see those assets depreciate by ~90% within five years — and no current business model recuperates that. Open-source models closing the gap every few months only compounds the pricing pressure, leaving frontier labs in a squeeze between massive capital costs and commoditizing margins.

On recursive self-improvement (RSI), Schmidhuber — who pioneered meta-learning and self-referential machines going back to 1987 — argues that RSI won't be a durable moat for any company. The key ideas behind RSI came from academic labs, not big companies, and PhD students worldwide are already building on them. "Everybody's cooking with the same water," he said flatly.

His most contrarian take may be on AGI itself: true AGI, he insists, requires physical embodiment in the real world. Text-behind-the-screen systems, however impressive, are "fancy text editors." Robot hardware remains far behind human physiology — no synthetic hand comes close to the sensor density and self-healing capability of the human hand — and he thinks matching that may take decades, not years.

On AI safety, Schmidhuber has long been the skeptic in the room. He never signed the safety letters of the 2010s, viewing the alignment framing as naive: "If you have 10 different humans in one room, they all will have different needs." He points to AI-controlled drones already fighting on both sides in Ukraine as evidence that universal alignment governance was always a fiction.

His long-term vision is optimistic though: robots that can learn to operate all existing human machines, replicate themselves, and eventually colonize the solar system — driven by "artificial curiosity," a reward signal for discovering new patterns at the edge of what a system already understands. That principle, he argues, is also the true engine of human creativity.

Direct quote: "All these companies are getting less and less efficient. So at some point, they wouldn't have to stop. Now they're taking on debt to finance even more data centers, and this will be possible only to a certain extent."
