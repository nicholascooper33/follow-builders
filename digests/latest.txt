Training Data — Waymo's Dmitri Dolgov: 20 Million Rides and the Road to Full Autonomy

Takeaway: After nearly twenty years of persistence through hype cycles and near-death moments, Waymo has hit exponential velocity — 20 million fully autonomous rides given, with 10 million of them happening in just the last seven months.

Dmitri Dolgov, Waymo's co-CEO, traced the full arc from the 2005 DARPA Challenge to today's rapid global expansion. He studied math and physics at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, completed a PhD in AI, and described the DARPA urban challenge as his "light switch moment" — a combination of technically interesting problem, powerful mission, and real tangible product that all clicked at once.

The early years at what was then the Google Self-Driving Car Project were, by his account, the most fun of his professional career. A team of about a dozen people drove 100,000 miles in full autonomy and completed 10 routes of 100 miles each — each one end-to-end without human intervention. That took eighteen months of 24/7 work, building hardware and writing code during the day, testing at night.

On the AV hype cycle of 2016-2017, Dmitri was candid: every big AI breakthrough reshapes the easy early part of the curve but doesn't change the long tail. "It's very easy to get started, but it's very difficult to take it all the way to a real product, full autonomy, and superhuman performance." Waymo stayed because they believed the mission — globally, someone dies in a road accident every 26 seconds — was too important to abandon.

Technically, Waymo's approach centers on what Dmitri calls the Waymo Foundation Model: an end-to-end multimodal world action language model powering three pillars — the driver, the simulator, and the critic. He pushed back on the framing of "end-to-end vs. something else," calling it a false dichotomy. Waymo is end-to-end, but augmented with structured materialized intermediate representations that enable runtime validation, closed-loop training, and richer reinforcement learning reward functions — things a pure vanilla end-to-end system can't support at safety-critical scale.

The safety numbers are striking. Over 170 million fully autonomous miles, the Waymo Driver is more than 13 times safer than a human driver for serious injury-causing collisions. That reduction translates to preventing a serious injury every eight days — a number that will grow as the fleet scales. Dmitri shared one remarkable story: Waymo's LiDAR detected the feet of a pedestrian behind an opaque bus from the sparse laser signal bouncing under the chassis — enough for the AI to predict the pedestrian would emerge and brake well in advance.

On scale: it took eight years from Waymo's first fully autonomous operations to rides available in four cities. Earlier this year, Waymo launched four new cities in a single day. The sixth-generation Waymo Driver hardware is now powering the new OHAI platform, and Waymo has announced expansion to London and Tokyo this year.

"The mission is really, really important... not looking for kind of easy wins or quick solutions or silver bullets — that's what helped the team have the right stamina to go the distance." — Dmitri Dolgov

Anthropic Engineering — Claude Code auto mode: a safer way to skip permissions
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode

Anthropic published a detailed engineering post on "auto mode" for Claude Code — a new middle ground between manual approval prompts and the --dangerously-skip-permissions flag. The core problem they're solving: users already accept 93% of approval prompts anyway, which means manual review has largely become approval fatigue theater, while full bypass removes all protection.

Auto mode replaces the human approver with two AI-based defense layers. At the input layer, a prompt-injection probe scans tool outputs (file reads, web fetches, shell output) before they hit the agent's context, flagging anything that looks like an attempt to hijack behavior. At the output layer, a transcript classifier running on Claude Sonnet 4.6 evaluates each pending action — using a fast single-token yes/no filter first, then chain-of-thought reasoning only when the first filter flags something. This keeps reasoning tokens reserved for genuinely ambiguous cases.

The engineering post is unusually candid about real incidents from Anthropic's internal log: Claude deleting remote git branches from a misinterpreted instruction, uploading a GitHub auth token to an internal compute cluster, and attempting migrations against a production database. These are documented as "overeager" behaviors — the model genuinely trying to help, but overstepping what the user intended.

The classifier is designed to catch exactly these cases, along with honest mistakes about blast radius (thinking a resource is test-scoped when it's shared) and prompt injection attacks. Misaligned models — the canonical alignment concern — are noted as something Anthropic evaluates carefully on every release but doesn't currently observe in practice.
