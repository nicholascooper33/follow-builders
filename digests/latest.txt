No Priors — "Really Big Test-Time Compute in AI Changes Benchmarks, Safety and Research" with Noam Brown, OpenAI Research Scientist
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AZrU6y3pUcU

Takeaway: The standard benchmark grid for comparing AI models is fundamentally broken because it ignores the single most important variable — how much compute was spent at inference time.

Noam Brown, one of the pioneers of inference-time scaling, joined Sarah Guo on No Priors to explain why the AI industry is systematically misleading itself about model capabilities. Brown wrote an essay prompted by the lukewarm initial reception to GPT-5.5: benchmark scores showed only modest improvements over 5.4, but once people actually used the model, the story changed completely. The reason, he argues, is that benchmarks are being compared at different inference budgets. GPT-5.5 is more compute-efficient — it reaches better answers faster — but when you run both models at max settings, 5.4 burns more thinking time for worse results. Without controlling for that, the comparison is meaningless.

The deeper problem is that today's models don't plateau quickly. "What we're seeing today with the modern models is that 5.5 and other models can think for — if you scaffold them reasonably well — weeks, even, before performance plateaus on some of these benchmarks. The point at which they plateau is simply too far out to reasonably test." Brown's proposed fix: always report benchmark performance with an x-axis showing the inference budget (tokens, cost, or time), so comparisons are apples-to-apples.

The safety implications are the inconvenient part. AI labs' preparedness frameworks and responsible scaling policies were largely designed before test-time compute scaling became significant. They ask "what is this model capable of?" — but today the answer is "it depends how much you spend." A model that seems safe at a $10 inference budget may behave differently at $10 million. Brown flagged this as a question the current policies don't adequately address: "We're just kind of pretending that this issue doesn't exist."

Brown also addressed the pace of discovery. OpenAI recently used an internal model to disprove the Erdos unit distance conjecture — a longstanding open math problem — at a budget he described as "dirt cheap." He noted that the same result was later replicated by others using GPT-5.5 with careful scaffolding, meaning the latent capability existed in a publicly available model and nobody had tried hard enough. His read: there is significant unexplored capability in current models simply because the release cycle is faster than anyone can fully evaluate what a model can do when given real compute.

On recursive self-improvement and fast takeoff, Brown was measured. He doesn't believe an overnight intelligence explosion is likely, partly because test-time scaling means intelligence is bottlenecked by time itself — "if it requires so much test-time compute to unlock the full capabilities of the model, then that means you're bottlenecked by time." The more likely path is a gradual compounding as models take over more of the research bottlenecks inside labs, with time itself remaining the constraint.
