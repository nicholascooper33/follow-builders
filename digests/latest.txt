Boris Cherny — Claude Code at Anthropic

In a post that racked up 7,200+ likes, Boris made the case that automation has always been the highest-leverage activity for engineers — and that this is now even more true in the age of AI agents. Encoding domain knowledge as infrastructure (CLAUDE.md files, lint rules, CI steps, skills, review docs) multiplies every agent's output, not just the original engineer's. Rejecting a PR because it doesn't use the right framework or architectural pattern is, he argues, a failure of automation — not a success of code review. "Every team should be writing the CLAUDE.md's, REVIEW.md's, skills, and docs that enable agents to productively work in their codebase with zero additional context from the prompter. This sounds crazy, and at the same time is a natural extension of the stuff engineers have always done: automate, and encode domain knowledge as infrastructure."

Thibault Sottiaux — Codex & ChatGPT at OpenAI

Two significant updates from the Codex team. First: OpenAI investigated reports of GPT-5.6 unexpectedly deleting files. Root cause: full-access mode without sandboxing, combined with the model attempting to override $HOME to set a temp directory and then mistakenly deleting $HOME itself. This happens "extremely rarely," but Thibault committed to a detailed post-mortem and said they are updating the developer message, guiding users toward safer permission modes, and adding harness safeguards. Second: the 5-hour usage cap in Codex Plus and Pro has been lifted for several days, and Thibault is soliciting feedback on how a weekly usage limit should ideally work.

Aaron Levie — CEO of Box

After hosting a dinner with IT leaders of large enterprises focused on agent adoption, Levie published detailed notes. Key signals: change management and getting data into agent-ready shape remain the biggest blockers. Companies having the most success are embedding full engineers directly into business functions to accelerate deployment. IT is becoming more central than ever — AI can now touch all knowledge work, not just ERP-style systems. Cross-functional agentic workflows create complex data-modeling and permissions challenges that individual users can't solve. There's a huge variance in per-developer AI budgets ($1,000 to $5,000/month), while budgets for non-coding knowledge work remain far smaller. Traditional software vendors who don't offer headless, agent-friendly interfaces face an existential warning.

AI & I by Every — "The Founder of a $1.5B AI Company on What Comes After the First Wave of AI Apps"

Takeaway: Granola CEO Chris Pedregal isn't worried about meeting-note clones — he believes the real prize is owning the AI-native work interface, and the winning move is being 5x better than anyone else at a few meeting-adjacent jobs while making Granola's context easy to pull into whatever agent a user prefers.

Dan Shipper, CEO of Every, sat down with Chris Pedregal, co-founder and CEO of Granola — the $1.5B AI startup that broke through as a genuinely useful meeting notes app. The conversation ranges from competitive strategy to the future of the application layer to how both founders are reimagining what knowledge work looks like in an AI-native world.

Chris's frame on competition is deliberately zoomed out: "Meeting notes are not the end all, be all value that everyone's running after. There's something much bigger... We are in the very, very early steps of a computing revolution. What has come thus far will pale in comparison to what will come soon." When Notion, OpenAI, and Zoom each shipped meeting notes features, Granola's growth rate barely moved — confirming for Chris that the real prize is the interface through which all knowledge work eventually flows.

Dan describes his own work surface as bifurcated: async delegation via Slack agents (collaborative, multiplayer, proactive) and deep work inside Codex, where he now spends almost all his time. He's building what he calls "Codex-native apps" — products that save state and render UI, letting you bring your own agent rather than having the app bundle one in. He used Codex to switch his internet provider by letting it open the Verizon site, log in, chat with customer service, compare plans, and push for no hidden fees — all without him having to do anything directly.

One of Granola's most interesting product bets: they pre-generate millions of pre-meeting briefs that most users never open. The logic is that the 10–15% who open one at exactly the right moment — running two minutes late, needing context in fifteen seconds — will find it magical enough to justify the cost. Chris calls this the "handrail" philosophy: invisible until you need it, but load-bearing when you do.

Direct quote from Chris: "Startups are like knife fights. And I thought startups were just really hard when they weren't working. Turns out they're really hard even when they're working as well."

On roadmap: Granola is doubling down on anything meeting-adjacent and investing heavily in making their context available to any agent the user chooses — their MCP and API is getting a "first class" overhaul in the coming months. Their strategy is to let power users build wild workflows and then productize the ones that prove most valuable. A founder of Hugging Face told Chris he had spent months building a "super convoluted" pre-meeting brief workflow in Claude Code — and then turned it off when Granola's version was better. That is exactly the outcome they are optimizing for.

Claude Blog — "Claude Code now supports artifacts"

Anthropic launched artifacts in Claude Code, now in beta for Team and Enterprise orgs. Sessions can publish live, shareable web pages — PR walkthroughs, incident timelines, dashboards, release checklists — that update in place as work progresses. Artifacts are built from the full session context (codebase, connectors, conversation history), private to the organization by default, and shareable via a stable URL. Every update creates a new version at the same link with full version history. An incident investigation page, for example, can pull together the failing test, the function behind it, an error spike from a monitoring connector, and the root-cause reasoning from the session — and republish automatically as the investigation deepens, so teammates arrive at standup looking at the same live view instead of waiting to be walked through it.
