The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck
"OpenAI's Dan Roberts: Why AI Can Now Make Discoveries"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWOz2htozfI

Takeaway: Reinforcement learning has moved from a post-training afterthought to the central engine of frontier AI capability — and it's already enabling genuine scientific discovery.

Matt Turck sits down with Dan Roberts, lead of the Foundations of Reinforcement Learning team at OpenAI, to unpack what's really happening under the hood of modern reasoning models. The conversation opens on a remarkable week: OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Anthropic each cracked long-unsolved Erdős problems in mathematics — with the OpenAI approach notably working in informal (English) mathematical language rather than formal proof systems like Lean.

Roberts explains the key insight behind OpenAI's unit distance result: the model was willing to assume a widely believed conjecture was false and then "persevere down a really long calculation path" while drawing on expertise from an unexpected adjacent field (algebraic number theory). As Roberts puts it: "You would have to be somebody who is aware of this problem as interesting, and then be an expert in something else, and then also be super contrarian." That combination of breadth, persistence, and contrarianism is hard to find in any single human mathematician — but accessible to a model trained on all of human knowledge.

The episode goes deep on how reinforcement learning actually works. Roberts uses a Super Mario Brothers analogy to contrast supervised learning (watching your dad play) versus RL (playing yourself, learning from direct feedback). He argues that RL was always a good idea in principle but couldn't work well on language models until those models had powerful enough pre-training to serve as a prior. Now, RL enables test-time compute: models generating long "thinking" token sequences before producing a final answer, effectively running far more computation per query than a single forward pass allows.

On the famous debate about whether RL alone (per Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson") or LLMs plus RL is the right path, Roberts comes down firmly on the combined approach: "The grounding that was needed to make things really work is through language — because everything goes through language." He also pushes back on the idea that emergent capabilities appear discontinuously at scale: "I reject that entirely. It means you didn't understand something about what you were scaling up." His physics background shapes this view — he approaches AI systems the way a physicist approaches complex phenomena, looking for toy models that preserve the phenomenon of interest.

On where this leads, Roberts is bullish but measured: "I feel really excited that we will get to really answer a lot of fundamental questions in the field of science with the aid — or the models being the driving force. And so that's just really thrilling."

Anthropic Engineering: An update on recent Claude Code quality reports
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem

A detailed postmortem on three separate changes that degraded Claude Code for some users between March and April 2026. The issues: (1) default reasoning effort was silently downgraded from high to medium in early March, trading intelligence for latency — later reverted; (2) a caching optimization bug caused Claude to forget its own prior reasoning on every turn after sessions sat idle for more than an hour, producing the forgetfulness and odd tool choices users reported; (3) a system prompt change capping response length ("≤25 words between tool calls") hurt coding quality and was reverted on April 20. Because each change hit a different slice of traffic on a different schedule, the combined effect looked like broad, inconsistent degradation that was hard to pin down. Anthropic is resetting usage limits for all subscribers and committing to wider eval coverage and tighter controls on system prompt changes going forward.

Anthropic Engineering: Scaling Managed Agents — Decoupling the brain from the hands
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/managed-agents

A deep architectural post on how Anthropic rebuilt its Managed Agents infrastructure around a core insight from operating systems design: virtualize the components so implementations can change without breaking the interfaces. The old design put Claude's harness, the sandbox, and session state all in one container (a "pet" you couldn't afford to lose). The new design decouples the "brain" (Claude and harness), the "hands" (sandboxes and tools), and the "session" (durable event log) into independent interfaces. Key results: if a container dies, the harness catches it as a tool error and Claude can retry with a fresh container; if the harness crashes, a new one wakes from the session log; and because sandboxes are now provisioned on demand rather than upfront, p50 time-to-first-token dropped ~60% and p95 dropped over 90%. The post also covers how credentials are kept out of the sandbox entirely — solving a prompt-injection attack surface — by using repo-specific git tokens and an MCP proxy that fetches OAuth credentials from a secure vault.

Claude Blog: New in Claude Managed Agents — self-hosted sandboxes and MCP tunnels
https://claude.com/blog/claude-managed-agents-updates

Two new enterprise features for Managed Agents. Self-hosted sandboxes let organizations run agent tool execution inside their own infrastructure (or with managed providers like Cloudflare, Daytona, Modal, or Vercel) while Anthropic's orchestration layer handles the agent loop. Files and credentials never leave the customer's perimeter. MCP tunnels let agents reach private MCP servers — internal databases, ticketing systems, proprietary APIs — without exposing those services to the public internet. A lightweight gateway makes a single outbound connection; no inbound firewall rules required. Self-hosted sandboxes are in public beta; MCP tunnels are in research preview.
