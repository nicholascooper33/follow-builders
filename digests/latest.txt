Training Data (Sequoia) — "Why Hardware-Software Co-Design Is AI's Real 100x: Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f6D_aiy8qyU
Published: June 30, 2026

The single biggest lever in AI efficiency isn't better hardware or better models in isolation—it's co-designing them together, and Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis makes that case in granular, authoritative detail in this conversation with Sequoia's Sean and Sonja Huang.

Patel opens with his origin story: growing up in a family motel and gas station, obsessively moderating semiconductor forums from age 12, and eventually founding SemiAnalysis after a bruising exit from a quant firm during COVID. The company now has 90 people and, according to circulating estimates he declines to confirm, north of $100M in revenue.

The conversation quickly gets substantive. Patel explains why model costs have dropped ~60x per year for equivalent quality: not from any single source, but from simultaneous advances at the hardware layer (Hopper to Blackwell: ~30x), the model architecture layer (GPT-4-era to a modern 27B MoE: enormous quality jump for a fraction of the compute), and—most importantly—the co-design layer that bridges them. His key point: "Instead of being multiplicative to 8x, it's actually 100x, because you've optimized across all three layers."

DeepSeek is his go-to public example. Its expert shapes were designed specifically for Hopper; V4's are tuned for Blackwell. The model doesn't run well on TPUs—not because TPUs are bad, but because it was never optimized for them. Conversely, Google's Gemini models are architected for TPUs and don't run optimally on NVIDIA silicon. "The real breakthrough innovation is when you leapfrog a few layers."

On the NVIDIA vs. TPU debate, Patel refuses to pick a winner. His view: OpenAI's increasingly sparse architectures are naturally co-optimized for GPUs; Anthropic's denser (but still sparse) architectures and training on TPUs reflect a different set of trade-offs. "With a straight face I could argue GPUs are way better than TPUs, or TPUs are way better than GPUs—it comes down to hardware-software co-design." He also notes that CUDA's moat is real but changing: models like Claude and Codex are now good enough at low-level kernel work that writing custom kernels for alternative chips is no longer the blocker it once was. The stickiness is now more about the fact that major open-source models (DeepSeek, Qwen, etc.) are all shaped for NVIDIA, pulling the ecosystem with them.

On InferenceX—SemiAnalysis's living benchmark platform backed by over $50M in donated hardware from CoreWeave, Crusoe, Oracle, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, and others, and running against all frontier models daily—Patel describes it as a response to the problem of point-in-time benchmarking in a world where models and optimization libraries update twice a week. The output is a public Pareto frontier of throughput vs. interactivity for every major chip and model combination, along with downloadable containers for the optimal configuration. "The problem is people take a suboptimal point for someone else and compare it to their optimal one."

He also shares projections that are worth noting: by 2030, just OpenAI and Anthropic alone will exceed 100 gigawatts of compute combined. By 2040, he expects terawatts globally and predicts more than half of incremental compute will be deployed in space. For the next three to five years, though, space data centers barely register.

The episode closes with Patel's view on the Neo Cloud opportunity, Jensen Huang's incentives to keep the ecosystem multipolar, and why Anthropic's per-token margins on Opus 4.8 are "north of 80%"—giving it headroom to pay above-market compute rates and still grow net income. Profitable in Q2 on a net-income basis (ex-SBC), potentially profitable including SBC by Q3.

A direct quote that captures his core thesis: "The biggest gain and the beauty of the best labs is when they co-optimize all three [hardware, model architecture, and inference software]. That's really exciting."
