The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck — "OpenAI Board Member Zico Kolter on the Real Risks of Frontier AI"
Published: May 7, 2026

Takeaway: AI safety is not a problem you solve by making models bigger — it requires explicit, ongoing investment in robustness, monitoring, and governance, at a pace that keeps up with the rapidly expanding attack surface.

Zico Kolter is head of the ML department at Carnegie Mellon and chairs OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee (SSC). In this conversation with Matt Turck, he gives an unusually candid and technically grounded tour of how AI safety actually works in 2026 — both inside OpenAI and across the industry.

On governance, Kolter explains that the SSC functions like an audit committee: it meets with safety teams before every major model release, reviews third-party evaluations, and can delay a release if it needs more information. He's careful to note this isn't about blocking progress — it's structured oversight that he believes other AI companies should adopt.

On the fundamental challenge of safety at scale: "So far, we have not seen that same thing happen when it comes to things like the robustness of models. You can't just sort of trust models to get safer by getting bigger." In other words, capability improvements come almost for free with scale; safety improvements do not. They require explicit safety training, input/output classifiers, and operational monitoring layered together — what Kolter calls a Swiss cheese model of defense, where no single layer is perfect but the combination makes breaches very difficult.

The conversation goes deep on jailbreaks. Kolter co-authored the influential 2023 GCG paper (Greedy Coordinate Gradient), which showed that algorithmically optimized nonsense-word suffixes could reliably bypass LLM guardrails — and that those same strings transferred to commercial models without modification. He explains that modern defenses have moved well beyond the model itself: classifiers on inputs, classifiers on outputs, safety-trained models, and traditional operational security (flagging accounts that probe boundaries repeatedly). Reasoning models, he adds, are much harder to jailbreak using the original GCG technique.

On agents specifically, Kolter identifies prompt injection as the defining new attack surface: malicious instructions embedded in third-party content (emails, web pages, tool call results) that the agent might follow as if they came from the user. His practical advice for builders: use sandbox environments, apply the principle of least privilege for agent permissions, and layer AI security on top of traditional cybersecurity hygiene.

He also shares an optimistic take on mechanistic interpretability: coding agents may be uniquely well-suited to doing the kind of systematic, large-scale pathway analysis that human researchers have always found tedious — potentially turning an ad hoc field into a real science.

The episode closes on his new intro AI course at CMU (somemodernaicourse.org), where undergrads build an LLM from scratch — including training and RL — in roughly 200–300 lines of Python. His point: these systems are far simpler than most people assume, and understanding them from the ground up is worth every builder's time.
