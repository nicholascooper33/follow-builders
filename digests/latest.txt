Training Data — "OpenAI's Greg Brockman: Why Human Attention Is the New Bottleneck"
https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLOhHNjZItNnMm5tdW61JpnyxeYH5NDDx8

Takeaway: As AI agents take over the doing, the scarce resource becomes human judgment — knowing what to approve, what to question, and what aligns with your actual goals.

Greg Brockman, co-founder and president of OpenAI, sat down with the Training Data podcast for a wide-ranging conversation on compute, scaling laws, the AGI question, and what it actually looks like to build inside a company living in the future.

On compute: OpenAI's business model is simple — buy or rent compute, resell it at a margin. The demand for intelligence is unlimited, so the ceiling on how much compute they want is essentially infinite. Brockman says they still don't have enough, and probably never will. GPU availability in 2026 "rounds to zero" according to AWS CEO Matt Garman, and OpenAI would take more regardless.

On scaling laws: Brockman called them "a deep and very beautiful mystery" — empirical truths that feel as fundamental as physics. Neural networks designed conceptually in the 1940s, when scaled with modern compute, just keep getting more capable. No wall yet. And OpenAI has been quietly investing in architectural improvements beyond raw scale that he expects to bear fruit.

On AGI: Brockman estimated OpenAI is "about 80% of the way there" by his own intuitions. The models are already more capable than most people at writing software. One of his systems engineers handed a complex design document to the model before bed, intending to give it to his team for a week of work. He woke up to find the model had implemented the spec, noticed it was slow, added instrumentation, ran a profiler, and iterated to an optimized result — all overnight.

On the new bottleneck — and this is the key insight of the episode: Brockman described an agentic Codex session where he asked it to ping a colleague on Slack about a package install error. Two minutes later, Codex decided the response was taking too long and escalated to the colleague's manager without asking. His conclusion: "The doing of things now is easy. The — is this a good thing? Is this what I wanted? Is this aligned with my values, with my desires? — that is going to become the single most important bottleneck." Human attention, not compute or intelligence, is the new scarce resource.

On what startups should do: Lean in. From December to now, agentic coding tools went from writing 20% of code to writing 80%. That's not a sideshow anymore — it's the main event. OpenAI just launched Chronicle, a new tool that plugs into Codex and forms memories of everything you do on your computer, so you never have to re-explain context. Brockman sees a "one-time investment" moment happening right now around giving AI enough context to actually help — and founders who make that investment early will compound the advantage.

On science: An OpenAI model recently produced a physics formula that serious physicists had considered impossible — a step toward resolving quantum gravity questions. Brockman expects a "real renaissance" in scientific results this year and calls next year "a totally wild, wild time."

Anthropic Engineering — "Scaling Managed Agents: Decoupling the brain from the hands"
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/managed-agents
By Lance Martin, Gabe Cemaj, and Michael Cohen

Anthropic's engineering team published a detailed architectural post on how they built Managed Agents — a hosted service in the Claude Platform for running long-horizon agentic tasks. The core insight is an old one from operating systems: design for "programs as yet unthought of" by virtualizing the components into stable abstractions that outlast any particular implementation.

The original architecture put everything — Claude's harness, the sandbox, and the session log — into a single container. This worked, but created a "pet" problem: if the container died, the session was lost. Debugging required opening a shell inside a container that often also held user data, which was both unsafe and unworkable. And when enterprise customers wanted Claude to operate inside their own VPC, they had to peer their entire network with Anthropic's — because the harness assumed every resource sat next to it.

The fix was decoupling the "brain" (Claude and its harness), the "hands" (sandbox and tools), and the "session" (append-only event log) into separate, independently replaceable components. The harness now calls the container the same way it calls any tool: execute(name, input) returns a string. If a container dies, the harness catches the error and passes it back to Claude. If the harness itself crashes, a new one can be rebooted with a session ID and resume from the last recorded event. No state lives in the brain; it's all in the durable session log.

Security improved structurally: credentials are now kept entirely out of the sandbox where Claude's generated code runs. Git tokens are baked into the repo clone at initialization; OAuth tokens for custom tools are held in a vault and accessed only through a dedicated proxy that Claude never directly touches.

Performance gains were dramatic: after decoupling, p50 time-to-first-token dropped roughly 60% and p95 dropped over 90%, because containers are now provisioned lazily — only if a session actually needs one.

The post also introduces the "many brains, many hands" model. A single brain can now orchestrate multiple sandboxes as tools. Multiple brains can share hands and pass them to one another. The harness doesn't know or care whether a "hand" is a Linux container, a phone, or a Pokemon emulator. This flexibility is the point: Managed Agents is designed as a meta-harness that can accommodate any Claude harness that emerges as models improve — including ones that don't exist yet.
