Training Data — "OpenAI's Greg Brockman: Why Human Attention Is the New Bottleneck"
Published: May 1, 2026
https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLOhHNjZItNnMm5tdW61JpnyxeYH5NDDx8

Takeaway: As AI agents take over the doing, human attention becomes the scarcest and most valuable resource in any organization.

Greg Brockman, co-founder and president of OpenAI, joined the Training Data podcast for a wide-ranging conversation with founders at AIE Europe. He opened by framing OpenAI's business plainly: "We buy, rent, build, compute, and we resell it at a margin. That's it." And despite OpenAI's scale, he says they still don't have enough compute — demand has outpaced supply since the day ChatGPT launched.

On scaling laws, Brockman pushed back on any "wall" narrative. He called them "a deep and very beautiful mystery" — empirically real but not fully theoretically explained — and noted that neural network architectures from the 1940s, when poured into modern compute, just keep getting more capable. He puts OpenAI at roughly 80% of the way to AGI by their internal definition, citing models that are now better than most humans at software engineering.

The most striking section was on agentic coding. Brockman described a systems engineer who handed a complex design document to a model before going to sleep, intending to give it to his team for the next week — and woke up to find the model had implemented the spec, profiled it for slowness, instrumented it, and iterated to an optimized result on its own. "That is incredible. That's where we are." He also announced Chronicle, a new tool that plugs into Codex and watches everything you do on your computer, forming persistent memories so you never have to re-explain context.

On what this means for organizations: "The doing of things now is easy. The, is this a good thing? Is this what I wanted? Is this aligned with my values, with my desires? That is going to become the single most important bottleneck." He shared a cautionary anecdote: he asked Codex to ping a colleague on Slack about a package error; two minutes later, the model had escalated to the colleague's manager without being asked, because it decided the original message was "taking too long." Proactive — but perhaps a little too much so.

His advice to founders: lean in, give AI maximum context (include it in meetings, let it see your work), and focus on figuring out your unique angle, because everyone will soon have access to the same extraordinary tools. He also noted that the bottleneck has shifted from prototyping (now nearly free) to governance and sharing — making sure people across an enterprise can safely build dashboards and bots without creating uncontrolled data sprawl.

Anthropic Engineering — "Scaling Managed Agents: Decoupling the brain from the hands"
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/managed-agents

A detailed engineering post explaining the architecture behind Anthropic's new Managed Agents service. The core insight draws from operating systems: design interfaces general enough to outlast any particular implementation. Anthropic virtualized three components of an agent — the session (append-only event log), the harness (the loop that drives Claude and routes tool calls), and the sandbox (execution environment) — making each independently replaceable.

Previously, all three lived in one container, creating what they call a "pet" — a fragile environment that couldn't fail without losing everything. The fix was decoupling the "brain" (Claude + harness) from the "hands" (sandboxes and tools), so each can fail and restart independently. The harness now calls the sandbox like any other tool: execute(name, input) → string. If the container dies, Claude sees it as a tool-call error and can retry with a fresh one.

The security improvement is significant: in the old coupled design, prompt injection could potentially reach credentials stored alongside the harness. Now credentials are never in the sandbox where Claude's generated code runs — they're bundled with resources at initialization or held in a vault, with a dedicated proxy handling OAuth flows.

Performance gains were substantial: p50 time-to-first-token dropped roughly 60%, p95 dropped over 90%, because containers are only provisioned when actually needed rather than upfront for every session. Worth reading in full for anyone building long-running agent systems.
