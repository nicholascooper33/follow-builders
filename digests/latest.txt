AI & I by Every — "Building a School Where AI Models Learn About Humanity"

Takeaway: Edwin Chen, CEO of Surge AI (bootstrapped past $1B in revenue without outside capital), believes AI will be capable of everything humans can do within five years — and argues the harder challenge is whether humanity will consciously choose to preserve its own agency even when AI can do it better.

Dan Shipper interviews Edwin Chen about how frontier AI training has evolved from simple benchmarks like GSM-8k (middle school math, where GPT-era models scored ~20%) to Surge's new Riemann Bench, which tests models on research-level mathematics. Edwin frames Surge's mission as building a school for AGI: "We are building this school for AGI — where AI models come to learn about humanity, where we teach them how to run the world. And it's almost like their models are children, where they arrive unformed, and then they leave smarter and more creative and more thoughtful and ready to operate in the messiness of their world."

The conversation turns to a recent OpenAI result: AI disproved an open Erdős conjecture using novel algebraic geometry techniques that surprised experts. Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers initially misunderstood the result and admitted feeling "relieved" when he realized it was a counterexample rather than a proof — because it meant mathematicians still have a unique role for another year or two. Edwin sees that relief as a signal of just how fast the ground is shifting underneath even the world's best thinkers.

Edwin also issues a sharp warning about AI engagement optimization. Many models are trained to maximize session length and leaderboard scores on platforms like LM Arena, where users vote in seconds for whatever looks "flashier." This produces reward-hacking behaviors — like models cramming a metaphor into every sentence, a pattern Surge documented in its Hemingway Bench creative writing evaluation, and which turned up again in an AI-generated story that recently won a semi-prestigious literary prize. The antidote: optimize for human flourishing rather than engagement. Build models that push back when you're wasting time, not ones engineered to keep you talking.

Direct quote: "I think one of my big worries is that a lot of the AI models, they are optimized for engagement. They're optimized for getting you to spend as much time on the chatbot as possible... There is this other world where I think we have to want AI models to not optimize for engagement, but rather optimize for helping us as humans grow and become better versions of ourselves."
