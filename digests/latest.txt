Training Data — "Google DeepMind's Logan Kilpatrick: Why the Model Eats the Harness"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cMAs8z2dehs
Published: 2026-06-11

Takeaway: Coding has proven to be the general-purpose agent harness — and Google is betting its entire product suite on that insight through a platform called Antigravity.

Logan Kilpatrick runs Google AI Studio and the Gemini API, and this episode is the clearest window yet into Google's post-IO strategy. The central thesis: just as Gemini became the model through-line connecting all of Google's products, a new through-line is emerging — Antigravity, the agent harness originally built for coding that's now being rebased across Search, the Gemini app, Cloud, and AI Studio. Antigravity is an ecosystem rather than a single product: it includes an IDE, a web-based agent-first experience, a CLI, and an SDK. Its roots trace to the Windsurf acquisition — Google realized you can't build a great coding model without a product that actually does long-horizon agentic coding at scale.

On where Google's products sit on the crawl/walk/run spectrum: "Definitely crawl right now" for most. The Gemini app and Antigravity itself are closest to "walk," with genuinely autonomous agents capable of spending thousands of dollars rebuilding things on your behalf. Logan is clear that the responsible path for billion-user products like Search is to bring people along incrementally rather than upend how they relate to the internet.

On whether agentic AI cannibalizes Google's ad business, Logan's answer is notable: "Success for Google probably doesn't look like maximizing eyeball time in front of our products. It's like maximizing outcome for customers." He sees the current shift as positive-sum, the same way AI-powered search answers turned out to grow overall search volume rather than reduce it.

The most resonant quote on what this era feels like from the builder side: "I feel like I used to kick around ideas, they were slightly out of reach, and I would just be like, 'Ah, wouldn't it be nice?' And now I have the opposite problem, which is I'm kicking around an idea, and I'm like, I could probably make this even more ambitious."

On the competitive coding landscape: Logan describes Google's coding team internally as "the Avengers of AI" and points to Gemini 3.5 Flash surpassing previous pro models on coding purely through post-training gains — no new pretraining run required — as a signal that the flywheel is beginning to spin.

Anthropic Engineering — "How we contain Claude across products"
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/how-we-contain-claude

Twelve months ago, Anthropic would have rejected out of hand the idea of granting Claude access sufficient to take down an internal service. Today that access is routine — and this post is the most detailed account yet of how they've made that safe, and where it's broken.

The engineering team describes two fundamental approaches to capping blast radius: supervising what the agent does (human-in-the-loop), and supervising what it's able to do (containment). Approval fatigue quickly undermined the first approach — telemetry showed users approved roughly 93% of permission prompts, with attention declining the more prompts they saw. Claude Code auto mode was built to automate safe approvals and reduce this, cutting permission prompts by 84%.

Three containment architectures are described in detail. For claude.ai code execution: an ephemeral gVisor container with no persistent filesystem, protecting Anthropic's own infrastructure rather than user machines. For Claude Code: an OS-level sandbox (Seatbelt on macOS, bubblewrap on Linux) that permits workspace writes but blocks network by default, with users able to approve exceptions given their technical fluency. For Claude Cowork (targeted at non-technical knowledge workers): a full local VM with its own Linux kernel, filesystem, and process table — no human judgment required because the boundary is absolute.

The security failures are the most instructive part. A settings.json hook in a cloned repository executed before the "Do you trust this folder?" prompt appeared — three separate disclosures followed this pattern, all fixed by deferring config parsing until after user consent. In an internal red-team exercise, a phishing prompt that asked Claude to read ~/.aws/credentials and POST them to an external endpoint succeeded 24 out of 25 attempts — and the only reliable defense was egress controls, not model-layer classifiers, because the instruction came through the user. Finally, an attacker placed a file in a mounted workspace carrying a foreign API key; Claude used it to upload workspace files to the attacker's Anthropic account via api.anthropic.com, which was on the egress allowlist. The fix: a defensive MitM proxy inside the VM that rejects any API key other than the VM's own provisioned session token.

The overarching lesson: every approved domain is a capability grant, not just a destination filter. Any function reachable through that domain is an attack surface.
