Unsupervised Learning — Ep 90: AI Pioneer Jürgen Schmidhuber on the State of AI Today
Host: Jacob Efron (investor at Redpoint)
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RKjR8DQ40po

Takeaway: Schmidhuber — credited by the New York Times and Forbes as a father of modern AI, inventor of LSTM and core meta-learning methods — is bullish on AI technology long-term but deeply skeptical of the current investment cycle, and believes closed-source model companies have no durable moat.

Schmidhuber opens by deflating hype around today's LLMs: passing the Turing test and beating benchmarks is real, but it isn't AGI. "You can't have AGI without hardware like that. You can't have AGI just behind the screen." Physical AI — robots that can operate in the real world with the dexterity of a human hand — is the missing piece. Robot hardware remains "really inferior compared to human bodies," and he estimates true physical AGI is decades away, not years.

On recursive self-improvement, Schmidhuber traces the lineage to his own work in the late 1980s and argues that the key algorithmic ideas all came from small academic labs — meaning large companies have no real moat there. Open-source models stay close enough to frontier closed models to keep constant pricing pressure. His prediction for the CapEx boom: a stock-market correction, not a civilization crash. "Just wait five years and compute is going to be 10 times cheaper. You will be able to do the same thing for one-tenth of the price." Companies investing at peak GPU prices while their free cash flows decline are, in his view, setting up a renormalization.

His more interesting long-term vision is what he calls "artificial scientists" — agents driven by artificial curiosity that generate their own training data through active experimentation rather than consuming whatever humans happened to post online. Such systems would be far less human-biased and would develop genuine understanding of the world through self-directed discovery, similar to how babies learn by predicting the consequences of their own movements. His "formal theory of fun and creativity" (2003) formalizes this: a scientist or curious agent seeks data where there is a pattern it didn't know but can quickly learn — "near the horizon of what you don't know and what you know."

On AI safety, Schmidhuber is notably less alarmed than many peers. He argues that truly intelligent systems with self-directed goals will be fascinated by life and motivated to protect it. He also dismisses the alignment-to-a-single-objective-function framing as naive — "if you have 10 different humans in one room, they all will have different opinions about what is good for humans." He closes with a vision of self-replicating robot societies that could eventually operate beyond Earth, and predicts that linear-complexity transformer variants will eventually displace today's quadratic transformers, which require exponentially more compute as context grows.

Direct quote: "You can't have AGI without hardware like that. You can't have AGI just behind the screen. You can have a superhuman chess player behind the screen and something that passes the Turing test. But if it doesn't master the real world, it's not an AGI."
