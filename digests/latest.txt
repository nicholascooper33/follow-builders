No Priors — "The Story Behind Cerebras' $63 Billion IPO with Founder and CEO Andrew Feldman"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jeop9wfb9jU

Takeaway: Cerebras bet from day one that radically better AI hardware required a radically different architecture — and then waited years for a market that eventually proved them right at a $63 billion valuation.

Andrew Feldman, co-founder and CEO of Cerebras, joined No Priors to tell the full story behind the company's IPO. The core thesis Cerebras built on: you cannot be 15-20x better than a GPU by making minor modifications to its design. So they built a wafer-scale chip — 46,000 square millimeters, about the size of a dinner plate — while everyone else was building chips the size of postage stamps. Industry veterans called it impossible. Gene Amdahl, one of the founding fathers of computing, had tried and failed.

Cerebras proved it was possible in 2019 after a brutal two-year stretch where the chip simply wouldn't yield. Feldman describes sitting in a small office in Los Altos, staring at a working computer, unable to speak for half an hour: "Nobody had been able to do this, and it's working. And we did this." Then came the second problem — nobody cared. AI was still a novelty, and fast inference doesn't matter when models aren't used in daily work. First-gen systems sold in dozens. Second-gen in the hundreds.

The company survived by targeting customers who valued speed regardless of software maturity: Argonne National Lab, Lawrence Livermore, Sandia, pharma, oil and gas. A billion-dollar order from sovereign partner G42 became the turning point — enough capital to transform their supply chain and battle-test at scale in real clusters.

Then 2025 hit. Models became smart enough to be genuinely useful every day, and the companies building on top — Cognition, Cursor, Lovable, OpenAI — ramped demand dramatically. Cerebras was right there with the fastest inference on the market, not by a little, but by 15-20x over GPUs across big models, small models, US models, Chinese models. They signed a deal with OpenAI worth north of $20 billion and an agreement with AWS to deploy in their data centers.

Feldman's framing for why speed compounds into a winner-take-all position: "How big is the market for slow search? It's zero. How big is the market for dial-up internet? It's zero. That's how big the market for slow inference will be."

Direct quote: "Netflix used to deliver DVDs in envelopes. And when the internet got fast, they became a movie studio. It opened up an entirely new business, something fundamentally different. That's what happens with speed."
