How Nuclear Will Unlock Energy Abundance with Valar Atomics Founder Isaiah Taylor
No Priors Podcast

Takeaway: Valar Atomics has done what no startup has done before — turned on an advanced nuclear reactor and powered an AI chip with it — and they believe the same hardware-iteration playbook that made SpaceX possible can make nuclear energy 10x cheaper for humanity.

This episode of No Priors was recorded live at Valar Atomics' nuclear facility in Utah with Isaiah Taylor, founder and CEO. The episode covers the full arc: why America stopped building reactors after Three Mile Island, why the nuclear industry became trapped in modeling-and-simulation rather than hardware iteration, and how Isaiah's background — his great-grandparents were both in the secret city during the Manhattan Project — gave him a lifelong belief in fission at scale.

Isaiah's core thesis is that nuclear has never had its "Ford moment" or its "Tesla moment." Reactors have been constructed, not manufactured. Valar's approach flips that: their reactors use TRISO fuel, which is inherently safe (no active cooling needed after shutdown), and are designed for mass manufacturing rather than one-off site construction. The goal is to bring costs down so dramatically that energy becomes cheap enough to create entirely new demand — not just serve existing demand.

The AI angle is central. Isaiah argues that AI compute is the largest new source of energy demand the world has ever seen, and the only way to serve it without constraint is abundant, cheap, carbon-free energy. He draws a direct line from AI chips needing power to nuclear being the highest-density clean energy source available. The milestone achieved — the first AI chip powered by a nuclear reactor, and the first TRISO reactor to turn on in over fifty years in the United States — was a proof point that the whole stack can work together.

On the regulatory environment: Isaiah is unusually bullish on the current moment, citing an openness to nuclear R&D that hasn't existed in decades. His argument is that the fear that froze the industry after Three Mile Island — where nobody actually died or was injured — created risk-aversion far out of proportion to actual danger. Valar is betting that hardware execution and regulatory tailwinds can unlock what fifty years of modeling and lobbying couldn't.

"Most of the nuclear industry is a modeling and simulation industry. They're not focused on hardware iteration, hardware execution, building the simplest and safest reactor that allows them to scale. And Valar just demonstrated that we are."
