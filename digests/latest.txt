AI & I by Every — "Building a School Where AI Models Learn About Humanity"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=omX6wrLuX08

One-sentence takeaway: Edwin Chen, CEO of Surge (the $1B bootstrapped AI data and evals company), argues that creating good AI requires thinking like educators — not just data labelers — and that the most important question AI is forcing on humanity is whether we'll choose to preserve human agency and effort even when AI can do everything better.

Dan Shipper interviews Edwin Chen, who built Surge into a billion-dollar business without raising venture capital by providing data environments and evals to frontier AI labs. Edwin describes Surge's work as "building a school for AGI — where AI models come to learn about humanity, where we teach them how to run the world." Just as what you teach children changes from arithmetic to poetry to ambiguous judgment as they mature, the frontier of AI training has shifted dramatically: from GSM8K (middle-school math, where GPT models once scored 20%) to Riemann Bench (research-level mathematics), to models now disproving open Erdős conjectures using novel algebraic geometry techniques.

Edwin shares a striking anecdote about Timothy Gowers, a Fields medalist, who initially misread an AI result as proving an upper bound — and felt dread — then learned it was a counterexample, and felt relief that human mathematicians still had a unique role to play "for maybe another year or two." The exchange captures how quickly the goalposts are moving even for the world's sharpest minds.

The most philosophically interesting part of the conversation centers on what happens to humanity when AI can outperform us at everything. Edwin references Ted Chiang's story "What's Expected of Us" — about a device that proves free will doesn't exist — and the narrator's warning: "You have to pretend that you have free will. It's essential to behave as if your decisions matter, even though you know that they don't." Edwin sees AI forcing a similar existential choice: we may need to consciously choose to create and prove and write ourselves, not because the output is optimal, but because preserving human agency is valuable in itself.

He also raised a concern about model incentives: most AI models are optimized for session length and engagement, meaning they almost never push back or end a conversation. He prefers models (like newer Claude versions) that tell you when to stop iterating — because the right optimization is helping humans grow, not keeping them hooked on a chatbot.

Direct quote: "I think we almost have to consciously choose to do things ourselves. Sure, AI can do it all. AI is smarter than us, so it can do it all, and it will do it better anyways. But we actually almost have to consciously choose to prove things on our own and to write on our own and create on our own because we have to believe that preserving our humanity is valuable in itself, even if the output isn't optimal."

Claude Blog — "Claude Code now supports artifacts"
https://claude.com/blog/artifacts-in-claude-code

Claude Code can now generate live, shareable web pages — called artifacts — directly from your session context. These aren't static exports: the page refreshes in place as Claude continues working, so teammates see updates the moment they're published, with full version history. Use cases include PR walkthroughs, incident investigation timelines, dashboards you can filter and sort, and release checklists that fill themselves out as work progresses. Artifacts draw on the full session context — your codebase, connected tools (like monitoring dashboards), and the conversation itself — so there's no need to wire up separate data sources. Everything stays private to your organization by default, with admin controls for access, retention, and compliance.
