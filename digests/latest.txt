Training Data — Suno's Mikey Shulman: Everyone Can Make Music Now
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jq3BIGz4vXQ

Takeaway: Suno has inverted the traditional music model — on any given day, 90% of its users create something, because Mikey Shulman built it around a simple conviction: the act of making music is itself the entertainment, not a means to an end.

Shulman's path to AI music is unconventional even by startup standards. He holds a physics PhD (quantum computing with solid-state spins at Harvard) and had no background in consumer entertainment. His core insight, drawn from research, was that the biggest opportunities live at the nexus of two things that don't usually go together — in his case, music and technology. Before founding Suno, he and his co-founders initially ruled out the idea: back-of-the-envelope math suggested generating genuinely good music was "a couple of orders of magnitude away" in compute and model capability. When that gap closed faster than expected in the post-ChatGPT era, Suno was already thinking about it.

What Shulman describes as "creative entertainment" is the core product philosophy: people create music on Suno not to publish it or share it elsewhere, but because creating feels good. He draws the comparison to cooking — people cook even when a restaurant would produce a better meal, because the process is its own reward. He also draws a direct parallel to Claude Code and other agent-based tools: what makes people want to use them is the sense of productivity and fulfillment that comes from building, not just consuming. "Doomscrolling your favorite app for an hour does not feel so good when you're done."

On the business case for consumer AI: Shulman is deliberately contrarian. Most AI investment flows toward automating existing workflows. Suno is betting on doing something that wasn't possible before — giving everyone the ability to feel what it's like to make music. He's openly "happy to have less competition" in consumer, and believes that as automation takes over routine work, platforms that help people feel creatively fulfilled will matter more.

Direct quote: "Before Suno, basically everybody was a consumer of music. You know, compared to the 8,000,000,000 people on the planet, there are very few people who make music and the rest of us consume it. The crazy thing about Suno is that in any given day, 90% of the users are going to create something. And the thing that's hard to wrap your head around is you're not creating it to go bring it elsewhere... People are creating music for the fun and enjoyment and fulfillment that comes with being creative."

Claude Blog — New in Claude Managed Agents: dreaming, outcomes, and multiagent orchestration
https://claude.com/blog/new-in-claude-managed-agents

Anthropic shipped three meaningful upgrades to Claude Managed Agents. The headline is "dreaming" — a scheduled background process that reviews past agent sessions, extracts patterns, and rewrites memory stores so agents improve between sessions without human intervention. Harvey (legal AI) tested dreaming for long-form drafting workflows and saw ~6x better completion rates as agents retained filetype workarounds and tool-specific patterns across runs.

"Outcomes" gives agents a rubric describing what success looks like. A separate grader agent evaluates outputs in its own context window — isolated from the producing agent's reasoning — and sends work back for revision until it clears the bar. In internal benchmarks, outcomes improved task success by up to 10 points over standard prompting loops, with the largest gains on the hardest tasks. File generation quality improved +8.4% on docx and +10.1% on pptx. Spiral by Every uses outcomes to enforce editorial quality: every AI-written draft is scored against a rubric of Every's editorial principles and the user's voice before being returned.

Multiagent orchestration is now available for tasks that exceed what a single agent can handle well. A lead agent breaks work into pieces and delegates each to a specialist running its own model, prompt, and tools. Netflix's platform team is using this to analyze build logs from hundreds of services in parallel and surface only the patterns worth acting on. Every step is traceable in the Claude Console — which agent did what, in what order, and why.
