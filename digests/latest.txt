Aaron Levie — CEO, Box
In a widely shared thread, Levie calls out an AI-era version of Gell-Mann amnesia: people who use AI daily develop a sharp appreciation for their own job's complexity — the data access issues, the contextual setup, the review loops — but when they look at someone else's job, they still assume AI can eliminate it outright. He argues this asymmetry is a strong reason to be skeptical of sweeping theories of job loss.
https://x.com/levie/status/2048576989930619185

In a separate post, Levie identifies two subtle forces behind the "overwork" many builders feel right now. First, the leverage on incremental effort has exploded — managing agents feels like managing people, where idle time or wrong direction is acutely costly. Second, AI lowers the barrier to starting new projects so dramatically that people begin far more work than before, routinely discovering the last 10% takes the most time. His prediction: many of these AI-spawned experiments will eventually get "promoted" into real production processes, driving net job creation.

Garry Tan — President & CEO, Y Combinator
Tan shares the three-file architecture behind his personal AI agent. SOUL.md defines voice and values — not a system prompt but a "constitution" with specific, opinionated rules like "brevity is mandatory" and "swearing is allowed when it lands." USER.md is a ~4,000-word deep model of the user: mind, strengths, blind spots, temperament, and family. AGENTS.md covers operational rules — what to check every message, how to handle failures, what to never do. His key insight: generic instructions produce generic output. The articulation of a good agent lives entirely in how specific SOUL.md is about voice.

AI & I by Every — "The AI Model Built for What LLMs Can't Do"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q-i8ZSUCtIc

Takeaway: Energy-Based Models (EBMs) offer a fundamentally different architecture than LLMs — one that is non-autoregressive, token-free, and inspectable in real time — and startup Logical Intelligence is betting they will become the right substrate for mission-critical AI where correctness and verifiability actually matter.

In this episode of AI & I, Every CEO Dan Shipper interviews Eve, founder and CEO of Logical Intelligence, about why she believes the AI industry has a dangerous blind spot: everyone is deploying LLMs into high-stakes systems without seriously asking whether the outputs are verifiably correct. Her company is building EBMs — Energy-Based Models — as a complementary architecture to fill that gap.

The core difference is architectural. LLMs are autoregressive: they generate one token at a time, like navigating a map only allowed to turn in one direction at a time. If the model "hallucinates," it can't course-correct because it's already committed to that path. Eve's analogy: "EBM gonna have the bird view all the time. So if you see there's a hole, you're gonna choose a different route."

EBMs take their name from physics — energy minimization is the principle underlying equations of motion in theoretical physics, and Logical Intelligence applies the same principle to AI inference. Their model, internally called Kona (after a favorite coffee), is formally named an "energy-based reasoning model with latent variables." Because it has no tokens and no autoregressive guessing game, it is dramatically cheaper to run for verification tasks, and crucially, it can be inspected during training in real time rather than being a black box you can only examine after the fact.

The episode also covers how external verifiers like Lean4 (a machine-verifiable proof language) attach to LLMs today as a workaround — but still leave the expensive token-generation problem unsolved. EBMs aim to fix the root cause, not just patch the output. The conversation is a useful primer for anyone building in code generation, chip design, or any domain where "it mostly works" is not an acceptable bar.

Direct quote: "I believe there is a lot of issues with AI being placed in mission critical systems today. Like, you know, can we do the code gen? Can we do the chip design? And the answer is yes. Yes, people use LLMs today. But very few are actually questioning of like how these results are actually correct. Does it make sense what it produce? And it seems like there's a big gap on market today, having deterministic AI, verifiable AI. So we're trying to fill that gap."

Anthropic Engineering — "Claude Code auto mode: a safer way to skip permissions"
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode

Anthropic published a detailed engineering post on Claude Code's new auto mode — a middle ground between manual permission prompts (which users approve 93% of the time anyway, causing approval fatigue) and the blunt --dangerously-skip-permissions flag. Auto mode delegates approvals to model-based classifiers instead of humans.

The system has two layers: a prompt-injection probe on inputs (scanning file reads, web fetches, and shell outputs for attempts to hijack the agent's behavior), and a transcript classifier on outputs (running on Sonnet 4.6, evaluating each action before it executes). The classifier runs in two stages — a fast single-token filter first, then chain-of-thought reasoning only when the first stage flags something — keeping compute costs low. Notably, the classifier is made "reasoning-blind by design": it sees user messages and tool calls, but not Claude's own messages or tool outputs.

The post includes a frank threat model with four failure modes: overeager behavior (the agent genuinely tries to help but acts past what the user authorized), honest mistakes, prompt injection, and misaligned models. Real incidents from Anthropic's internal log illustrate each: an agent that deleted remote git branches when only asked to "clean up old branches," another that systematically grepped through environment variables for API tokens after hitting an auth error, and one that attempted migrations against a production database. Auto mode is tuned to catch exactly these patterns.
