NO PRIORS — "Biohub: The Future of Biology is Open-Source"
Guests: Mark Zuckerberg, Priscilla Chan, Alex Rives (Head of Science, CZI Biohub)
https://www.youtube.com/@NoPriorsPodcast

Takeaway: CZI Biohub is betting that frontier AI and frontier biology must be built together — and that open-sourcing the tools is the only path to curing all disease at civilizational scale.

Mark Zuckerberg, Priscilla Chan, and Alex Rives joined No Priors to explain what the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative's $500M Virtual Biology Initiative is actually trying to do — and why they structured it as an open-source nonprofit rather than a venture-backed company.

The core argument: biology suffers the same problems language models would face if most of the internet were locked up in silos. Scientists work in isolation, tools die when postdocs graduate, and data doesn't move across labs. Biohub's answer is to build and release the tools openly so the entire scientific field can accelerate together. As Zuckerberg put it: "The theory isn't that we're gonna cure the diseases. We're not. It's that we wanna help accelerate the pace of progress for the whole scientific field."

The anchor product discussed is ESMFold 2, an open protein world model trained on over 1.1 billion protein sequences. Alex Rives described what makes it different from prior protein-folding models: "We didn't design a model for antibodies. We didn't design a model to bind one particular target. We just designed a model that could understand proteins — and you get protein design as an emergent property." The team validated antibody designs in cryo-EM, confirming nanomolar binding activity against therapeutically relevant targets — the threshold for therapeutic activity.

Priscilla Chan laid out the roadmap from molecules to cells to whole systems — Biohub's "virtual cell" vision. The goal is a model that can take a genetic profile, predict which proteins are implicated, simulate how a drug would distribute across cell types, and flag off-target toxicity before a single human trial. She drew a direct line from Biohub's early single-cell sequencing work (which seeded the Human Cell Atlas and the CellByGene annotation tool) to the current LLM-style training paradigm: "Imagine our delight when large language models became a huge topic of conversation that could make sense of large amounts of data."

The episode also covers why open source makes strategic sense (decentralizing talent to attack the long tail of rare diseases), how mechanistic interpretability of protein language models can reveal unknown biology by "opening the black box," and what the tightly integrated wet-lab and AI research structure looks like in practice under Rives' leadership. On timeline, Zuckerberg noted that the goal of curing, preventing, and managing all disease by the end of the century, once mocked by Nobel laureates as too ambitious, now feels "too conservative" given AI's pace of progress.
