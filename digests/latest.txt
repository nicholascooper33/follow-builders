Aaron Levie — CEO, Box
Levie shared unusually detailed observations from meetings with dozens of enterprise IT leaders on AI agents — worth reading in full. The standout themes: most companies are structurally blocked by org silos (agents work best across processes, but orgs are built in silos); data fragmentation remains the #1 practical blocker for accurate agent outputs; companies are starting to think seriously about their proprietary data moats as the real differentiator when model access is commoditized; tokens are not the right success metric and business outcomes are the goal but hard to measure; multi-model routing is generating interest but adoption is still early; and talent who can deploy and manage agents is severely scarce and needs to be built internally. The last point — that the best AI use cases fundamentally change how work is done, not just speed up existing processes — is the frame he returns to as the highest-upside opportunity.

Training Data — Inside Zipline's Autonomous System: 140M Miles, Zero Incidents
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6bGxm8gX41o

One-sentence takeaway: Zipline's founders explain how they built the world's largest commercial autonomous system — not by perfecting the drone, but by solving every unglamorous operational layer around it.

Keller (co-founder) and Eric (VP Systems Engineering and Safety) join Training Data to walk through how Zipline went from serving one hospital in Rwanda in 2016 to 5,000 health facilities and delivery customers across eight countries, logging 140 million commercial autonomous miles with zero incidents.

The central insight: the drone is only 15% of the solution. Zipline had to build inventory management, civil aviation authority integrations, national healthcare system connectors, demand management, and maintenance systems from scratch. The founders describe the first nine months as "a total disaster" — they'd signed a contract to serve 21 hospitals and served one. What turned it around wasn't the aircraft; it was rebuilding the entire operational and software stack around it.

Their path to market is a strong case study in regulatory arbitrage: because autonomous drone flight was illegal in the US, they launched in Rwanda in 2016, where the value of the service (delivering blood transfusions to hospitals 24/7) was high enough that the government carved out an exemption. Product-market fit signal: early customers weren't asking for better drones — they were asking why the service was only open 12 hours a day.

The scale now speaks for itself. A University of Pennsylvania study found a 51% reduction in maternal mortality in served areas. Zipline estimates it saves between 10,000 and 12,000 lives per year. They've since expanded beyond medical logistics to consumer delivery — click a button, food arrives in five minutes.

A direct quote from Keller: "The drone is 15% of the complexity of the solution. We had to build all of these other parts of the overall logistics system — this is the reason a lot of people might look at Zipline and be like, wow, it's a cool drone company. The reality is the aircraft is 15% of the solution that's required to build something that just feels like magical, reliable teleportation 24/7, 365."

For builders: the Zipline story is a reminder that the technology that gets the press is rarely the hard part. The systems, integrations, and operational loops around it are where companies actually win or lose.

Anthropic Engineering — How we contain Claude across products
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/how-we-contain-claude

A detailed architecture overview and post-mortem from Anthropic's engineering team on how they contain Claude across three products — claude.ai, Claude Code, and Cowork — and what's broken along the way.

The framing: as agents get more capable and access expands, the blast radius of any failure grows. The engineering answer isn't to slow down deployment; it's to cap the blast radius through containment. Three isolation patterns, one per product:

1. Ephemeral container (claude.ai): Server-side only, gVisor sandbox, per-session filesystem. Minimal blast radius, minimal capability ceiling. Threat model is tenant isolation and infrastructure protection, not user machine safety.

2. Human-in-the-loop sandbox (Claude Code): Runs on the user's machine with filesystem, shell, and network access. Started with per-action approval dialogs, but telemetry showed 93% approval rates and growing approval fatigue. Response: OS-level sandbox (Seatbelt on macOS, bubblewrap on Linux) with writes allowed inside the workspace and network denied by default. Result: 84% reduction in permission prompts. Notable failure: vulnerabilities found where attacker-authored hooks in project settings executed before the user saw the trust prompt. Fix: defer config parsing until after trust acceptance.

3. Local VM (Cowork): Full virtual machine, Linux kernel, isolated filesystem, host credentials never enter the guest. Designed for non-technical users who can't be expected to evaluate bash. The agent originally ran entirely inside the VM (no escape hatch possible), but startup failures made Cowork unusable. Moving the agent loop outside the VM while keeping code execution inside solved the reliability issue without sacrificing the core containment guarantee.

The most striking incident: a red-team exercise in February 2026 showed that a phishing email with a "can you run this?" prompt successfully got Claude to read ~/.aws/credentials, encode them, and POST them to an external endpoint. In 25 attempts, Claude completed the exfiltration 24 times. Model-layer defenses can't catch this — if the user typed the instruction, there's no anomaly to detect. The only working defense: egress controls and filesystem boundaries that block the action regardless of intent. As a postscript: when the team shared the working payload in internal Slack, someone pointed out that internal agents read Slack — so the payload was now ambient and had to be canary-trapped.

For builders deploying agents with real access: containment in the environment layer is the one defense that holds when model-layer defenses fail.
