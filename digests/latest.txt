No Priors — Re-engineering the Semiconductor Supply Chain with Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan
Published: June 18, 2026
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=asCgCv2XB4s

Takeaway: Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan is betting that the agentic AI boom is precisely what the company needed — CPUs are back in demand, and Tan is methodically rebuilding Intel's culture, balance sheet, and foundry business to capitalize on the moment.

Tan, 66 and a 13-year veteran CEO of Cadence, took what he calls "the hardest job in the industry" out of conviction that Intel is too important to the US semiconductor ecosystem to let fail. His first fourteen months have been anything but smooth: President Trump initially asked him to resign over a conflict-of-interest concern, before Tan got a hearing and persuaded him otherwise. He framed the episode with characteristic equanimity — "I don't need this job. I do it purely to save Intel."

His turnaround playbook is disciplined and sequential: strengthen the balance sheet first (the US government is now a major shareholder, mirroring how Taiwan's government backed TSMC at the start), simplify the product line, get engineering to report directly to the CEO, and drive faster decision-making. Jensen Huang's $5 billion investment — which Tan notes has since grown to $25 billion or more — validates the direction. On culture, Tan is pushing hard against Intel's historical bureaucracy: "I'm so used to startup culture, you move fast in the speed of light."

What makes the timing interesting for AI builders: Tan argues the agentic AI wave is shifting the GPU-to-CPU ratio. Where training clusters ran 8 GPUs for every 1 CPU, inference and agent orchestration workloads can run as close as 1-to-1. "In terms of reinforced learning, in terms of the speed of orchestrating all the agents, the CPU is actually better," he said.

On Intel's foundry business, Tan is adamant it's a service and trust business: yield, defect density, and cycle time are non-negotiables. He's doing all senior recruiting himself — no search firms. The episode closes with a long view on what winning looks like over the next decade: a full-stack Intel delivering silicon, software, and complete rack systems.

Direct quote: "Jensen Huang, my all time friend, he also put $5,000,000,000 in investing and support me. His $5,000,000,000 become $25,000,000,000 now."

Anthropic Engineering — An update on recent Claude Code quality reports
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem

Anthropic traced a wave of user-reported quality degradation to three separate, overlapping changes rather than any single regression. All three are now fixed as of April 20 (v2.1.116); the API was unaffected throughout.

The three issues: (1) On March 4, Claude Code's default reasoning effort was quietly lowered from high to medium to reduce latency — a tradeoff users rejected, and Anthropic reverted on April 7. (2) On March 26, a bug caused Claude's thinking to be wiped every turn (not just once after an idle hour as intended), making the model seem forgetful and repetitive — fixed April 10. (3) On April 16, a verbosity-reduction system prompt was added that, combined with other prompt changes, degraded coding quality — reverted April 20.

Because each change hit a different slice of traffic on a different schedule, the combined effect looked like broad, inconsistent degradation that was hard to distinguish from normal feedback noise. Anthropic is resetting usage limits for all subscribers as of April 23 in response.

Anthropic Engineering — Scaling Managed Agents: Decoupling the brain from the hands
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/managed-agents

Anthropic's key insight in building Managed Agents: harnesses encode assumptions about what the model can't do on its own, but those assumptions go stale as models improve — so the architecture needs to outlast any particular implementation.

The solution draws on a classical computing analogy: just as operating systems virtualized hardware into abstractions (process, file) general enough for programs not yet written, Managed Agents virtualizes agent components into three interfaces — session (an append-only log of everything that happened), harness (the loop that calls Claude and routes tool calls), and sandbox (the execution environment). Each can be swapped independently.

The team learned early not to "adopt a pet": bundling session, harness, and sandbox into a single container made that container irreplaceable. Failures required nursing rather than replacement. Decoupling the brain (Claude) from the hands (tools, sandbox) means a stuck sandbox can be replaced without losing the session, and a smarter model can be dropped in without rewriting the harness.

Claude.com Blog — New in Claude Managed Agents: self-hosted sandboxes and MCP tunnels
Published: May 19, 2026
https://claude.com/blog/claude-managed-agents-updates

Claude Managed Agents can now run tool execution inside infrastructure you control. The agent loop (orchestration, context management, error recovery) stays on Anthropic's servers, while the sandbox where the agent actually runs code moves into your perimeter — on your own infrastructure or with managed providers including Cloudflare, Daytona, Modal, and Vercel.

The practical upshot: sensitive files, packages, and internal services never leave your network. Your existing security policies, audit logging, and network controls apply automatically. You also control compute sizing, which matters for agents doing long builds or image generation.

Alongside self-hosted sandboxes, MCP tunnels (in research preview) let agents connect to private MCP servers inside your enterprise. Real-world deployments already using the new capabilities: Amplitude built a Design Agent for on-brand UI production on Managed Agents and Cloudflare; Clay's GTM engineering agent, Sculptor, builds, tests, and monitors workflows on Managed Agents and Daytona. Self-hosted sandboxes are in public beta on the Claude Platform.
