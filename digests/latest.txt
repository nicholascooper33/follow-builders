Madhu Guru (former Product Leader at Google for Gemini, Veo, and Nano) laid out three phases of how enterprises are approaching model routing. Phase 1 (2024): everyone defaulted to the "it" model (GPT), regardless of task. Phase 2 (early 2025): teams over-corrected and chased the smallest/cheapest model but lacked evals to do it right, slowing down shipping. Phase 3: the most sophisticated AI-native startups broke their products into sub-agents and routed each task to the right model — e.g. hardest reasoning to Claude, simplest tasks to Gemini Flash-Lite or open-weight models. Enterprises followed the AI-native builders 6-9 months later.

Unsupervised Learning — Ep 89: AI Research Legend's Honest Assessment of Where We Are
Guest: Lukasz Kaiser (co-author of "Attention Is All You Need," formerly Google Brain and OpenAI)
Host: Jacob Efron (investor at Redpoint)
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N1geOimmdDo

Takeaway: One of the original transformer architects gives an unflinching look at where current AI excels, where it still has fundamental gaps, and why this is still the most exciting era to be an ML researcher.

Lukasz Kaiser opens with a nuanced take on the generalization question that dominates AI discourse: transformers with reasoning and agents can do "amazing things," but there is a distinct feeling that the current architecture is not quite there yet. He puts it vividly: "LLMs, they will learn the concept — but after exhausting all other options. You need a trillion tokens... That's not how we learn." He's not dismissing transformers — he's impressed every day — but he believes there is likely "something else" that could generalize better from far less data, a feeling shared by multiple post-transformer labs now exploring new architectures.

On agentic coding, Kaiser is emphatic: using agents has changed his work more than anything in the last 15–20 years. He estimates roughly a 10x speed-up on paper reproduction tasks, but the more interesting effect is qualitative — working with agents keeps him thinking at the architecture and loss level rather than getting buried in function names and class structure. "It's this beautiful thing where you can just be in this flow. You just think machine learning wise, what's supposed to happen. You tell it, verify it, and it's happening."

On Anthropic's early lead in coding, Kaiser is direct: "Anthropic made this very good decision to focus on coding. OpenAI was like, we're doing ChatGPT... Partly Anthropic made this decision was that they just could not compete." He credits the strategic bet, not a technical one-time advantage.

On the mysterious Christmas 2025 coding breakthrough, he admits he can't fully explain it — and suspects most people can't: "It was not like a big pretraining that changed it that much... the change last Christmas, it's a little hard to pin down." He sees it as a combination of harness improvements, post-training tweaks, and new base models all clicking together.

Kaiser closes with an invitation to researchers everywhere: with a modern GPU (he bought an RTX 5090 — roughly 200 TFLOPS, compared to 9 TFLOPS on the GPUs used for the original transformer work), you can run ML experiments that would have required an 8-GPU lab machine just a few years ago. He urges the field to publish more wild ideas and explore directions that fall outside the current paradigm. "You need to explore the wrong things because they may lead you to the right thing."
