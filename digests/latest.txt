Dan Shipper — CEO, Every

Shipper published a conceptual framework for understanding how people adapt when AI takes over their work. He describes three stages: (1) Initial agency rupture — the AI's contribution is highlighted and human scaffolding is invisible ("AI solved the Erdős problem"); (2) Seeing the scaffolding — you start noticing what's actually required of humans; and (3) Agency reconstruction — the human contribution is re-centered and the AI becomes invisible ("I did this"). His conclusion: "the ability to metabolize agency ruptures and turn them into playfulness and curiosity is a good leading indicator for whether you're a person who likes and excels in the new AI economy."

Aaron Levie — CEO, Box

Levie wrote a detailed thread on why some of the "hardest" work — math, code, cybersecurity — is actually most prone to automation first, because it is verifiable. Fields like legal, marketing, and sales don't have one right answer, rely on shifting context and human judgment, and can't be tested at scale. His conclusion: even as model capability grows exponentially, there will remain substantial value at the applied AI layer, and business processes themselves may need to change to unlock full automation gains. "We may even need all new capabilities to be able to 'test' knowledge work over time as we have had with software."

Building the Automated AGI Lab: Core Automation's Jerry Tworek and Rohan Anil
Training Data Podcast
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2RJiaf0SY8s

One-sentence takeaway: Core Automation's founders argue the transformer has hit a fundamental ceiling — it cannot do continual learning — and that building the most automated AI research lab in the world is the fastest path to finding what comes next.

Jerry Tworek (formerly VP at OpenAI, where he ran the Strawberry and reasoning teams) and Rohan Anil (one of four pre-training leads on Gemini, and a research lead at Google Brain and Anthropic) joined the Training Data podcast to explain why they left the frontier labs to start Core Automation.

Jerry's core thesis: while scaling pre-training and reinforcement learning has produced remarkable models, the real-world task distribution is far messier than any training dataset can capture. Models trained in the lab and deployed in the wild keep hitting the same ceiling — they can't adapt. The bottleneck isn't data or compute; it's the architecture. Transformers are fundamentally incapable of continual learning. In-context learning is too short-lived (Codex context runs out after ~20 minutes), and fine-tuning suffers from catastrophic forgetting. Jerry's definition of AGI: "a model that can improve itself without a human in the loop in any way." His read: the current path doesn't get us there.

Rohan adds the optimization angle. Pre-training and RL are currently treated as separate processes, but combining them end-to-end could unlock order-of-magnitude efficiency gains. He also points to the transformer's shallow computational depth as a structural weakness — chain-of-thought reasoning buys more depth one token at a time, but it's inefficient at inference. Their near-term roadmap centers on automating kernel generation: the QR factorization kernel written during their GPU Mode competition runs 60x faster than off-the-shelf solvers, but achieving it currently requires three specialists, $100k in coding-agent compute, and four weeks. Getting AI to do that reliably is their inner loop to testing new architectures.

"Core Automation is a lab created to build models that continuously learn and learn from deployment. We believe that transformers are incapable of continual learning. There's no way to put continual learning on transformers." — Jerry Tworek

On the market timing question — why start a company rather than work inside a big lab? Jerry's answer: the biggest labs are in the most competitive release cycles ever, which makes it hard for them to invest in paths that won't pay off this quarter. Everyone is training the same coding agent. Core Automation is trying to fill a different niche. Their aspiration for the lab's automation level is vivid: "We go on vacation as a team and see if the lab produces something better for the week. Then we extend the vacation two times, four times — until we are on permanent vacation."
