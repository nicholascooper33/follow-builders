No Priors — "Why Traditional Benchmarks Fail Modern AI Models with OpenAI Research Scientist Noam Brown"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AZrU6y3pUcU

Takeaway: Current AI benchmarks are dangerously misleading because they ignore test-time compute — the fact that model capability is now a function of how much money and time you throw at the problem.

Noam Brown, one of the pioneers of inference-time scaling at OpenAI, joined Sarah Gore on No Priors to explain why the standard benchmark grid — a table showing each model's score on a fixed set of tasks — has become inadequate for evaluating modern AI. The core problem: when GPT-3 was released, giving it a $10 million inference budget barely moved the needle. Today, the same model can perform dramatically better with more compute, and some models can continue improving on tasks even after running for weeks. The benchmark grid doesn't capture any of this variation.

Brown used GPT-5.5 as the clearest example. When 5.5 launched, initial benchmark comparisons showed only modest gains over 5.4, and skepticism spread quickly. But that skepticism missed the point: 5.5 is far more efficient with its thinking — it reaches better answers faster, using less compute. Once you normalize for compute budget, the improvement is substantial. The grid made it look close because it didn't hold compute constant.

His proposed fix is straightforward: benchmarks should include an x-axis — whether that's tokens, cost, or time — so you can compare models under equivalent conditions. The industry knows this is a better approach, Brown says, but nobody wants to be first to stop publishing the familiar grid. It's a bad equilibrium everyone has tacitly accepted.

The safety implications are the most urgent part of the essay Brown wrote to accompany this discussion. Responsible scaling policies and preparedness frameworks at the major labs were designed before test-time scaling was a significant factor. But if a model's ability to assist in dangerous tasks can be dramatically enhanced by allocating more budget, then capability evaluations need to account for that. Most current frameworks don't, and Brown sees this as an inconvenient truth the industry is not yet confronting head-on.

Brown also discussed the remarkable case of OpenAI's internal model disproving the Erdős unit distance conjecture — a longstanding open math problem — at surprisingly low cost. He used it to argue that there is significant latent capability in already-released models that nobody has fully explored, because the model release cycle moves so fast that few users have had time to push current models to their limits before the next one arrives.

On recursive self-improvement and fast takeoff scenarios, Brown is measured: he doesn't see an overnight intelligence explosion as likely, partly because large-scale test-time compute creates a time bottleneck. You can't compress weeks of model reasoning into a moment. For now, the biggest constraint for every frontier lab is simply hours in the day.

Direct quote — Noam Brown:
"The problem is we're in a world now where the capability of the model is a function of how much money you put into it, basically. If you give it a budget of $10,000, it can do a lot more than what it can do with a budget of $10. Give it a budget of $10,000,000, it can do even more. And so at what budget should you evaluate these models? The policies that exist today don't really address that question."
