The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck
"Why AWS and Azure Cannot Run Autonomous AI – Ivan Burazin (Daytona)"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kMXJrzAa5fM

Takeaway: AWS and Azure were built for stateless, ephemeral workloads — and that foundational architecture is precisely why they cannot natively support the stateful, long-running sandbox environments that AI agents actually need.

Ivan Burazin is the CEO of Daytona, the agent infrastructure startup known for building "composable computers" for AI agents. In this dense, wide-ranging episode, Matt Turck walks Ivan through the full agent stack — models, sandboxes, tools, memory, orchestration, observability — and probes why the infrastructure problem underneath AI agents is harder than it looks.

Ivan's core thesis is clean: agents are digital knowledge workers, and like any knowledge worker, they need a computer. A sandbox is that computer — a full, isolated environment where an agent can install tools, browse the web, run code, and access files. What makes this different from spinning up an EC2 instance is the state problem. AWS was architected for stateless apps — you don't want the button position on eBay's checkout page changing on the fly. Agents, by contrast, need stateful, long-running machines that can be paused, resumed, forked, and snapshotted mid-task.

On the architecture: Daytona sandboxes spin up in 60 milliseconds, 50,000 can be created in 70 seconds, and the company has customers running billions per day. They built their own scheduler from scratch because Kubernetes and Nomad weren't designed for this workload pattern. And unlike most sandbox providers who route disk I/O over a network drive (hundreds of thousands of IOPS), Daytona uses local disk — tens of millions of IOPS — which matters when agents are doing heavy file and compute work.

Ivan described a vivid real-world scenario that crystallized the isolation argument: "I asked Claude, can you go fetch the data from our bank? And then it was like, 'Oh yeah, just log in and give me access.' I'm like — no. I will not give you access." The solution: give the agent its own Daytona account, its own phone number for 2FA, its own isolated machine with a $100/day cap on the credit card. The worst-case risk shrinks to data leakage, not unauthorized transactions.

The episode also covers the coming CPU shortage. As reinforcement learning drives most model improvement and every agent deployment needs compute, CPUs are becoming scarce faster than people expect. Ivan pointed to a Semi Analysis report from Dylan Patel projecting shortages around October. Daytona has designed its scheduler to attach to any bare-metal provider to stay ahead of the constraint.

On memory — one of the most under-solved layers in the agent stack — Ivan is candid: "The best solution right now is just dumping things into MD files." He agreed that feels "quite trivial for the problem that's there," but solving it isn't in Daytona's mandate; making the file system fast and accessible to agents is.

There's also a standout GTM section. Ivan overcame severe stage fright by accidentally becoming the emcee for a 1,950-person conference in Croatia after the hired emcee bailed last minute. That forced him to understand events as entertainment first — and everything else (speakers, audience, sponsors) as incentive structures underneath. Daytona has no salespeople. The entire motion is PLG, Twitter, and in-person meetups and conferences, including a recent event at the Chase Center in San Francisco. Ivan's go-to-market philosophy: "If all things were equal and our product is equal to everyone else's, what is the differentiator? Can more people know about you? Is the branding there? Is the experience there?"
