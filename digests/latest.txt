No Priors — "Re-engineering the Semiconductor Supply Chain with Intel CEO Lip Bu Tan"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=asCgCv2XB4s
Published: June 18, 2026

Takeaway: Lip Bu Tan is running a disciplined, decade-long turnaround at Intel — and agentic AI is unexpectedly working in his favor, driving CPU demand back to the center of the AI stack.

Fourteen months into his tenure as Intel CEO, Lip Bu Tan sat down with the No Priors hosts to walk through what's changed, what hasn't, and where he's betting next. The conversation covered Intel's foundry ambitions, a surprising collaboration with Elon Musk on TeraFab, and a venture-capital-at-heart philosophy that shapes every decision he makes.

Lip Bu opened by explaining why he took the job at 66 when most expected him to retire: "This is an iconic company, and it's so important for the semiconductor ecosystem and also so important for United States." He laid out a "crawl, walk, run" framework — first strengthening the balance sheet, then simplifying products, then building toward the next generation. The US government becoming a major shareholder was part of the balance sheet fix: "I explained to President Trump, TSMC, when they started, they had the Taiwan government as a shareholder."

On the AI infrastructure moment, he pushed back on the assumption that GPUs dominate everything: "In terms of reinforced learning, in terms of the speed of orchestrating all the agents, the CPU is actually better." He sees Intel's Xeon (Influence CPU) benefiting directly from the agentic AI wave, with a supply-demand ratio shifting from one CPU per eight GPUs toward one-to-one.

The TeraFab partnership with Elon Musk came up at length. Lip Bu described Musk as "unconventional" in the best sense — questioning every assumption about how fabs have traditionally operated. Intel is collaborating on process technology and production, while Musk's team builds its own facility. "He's a very good team that I work with weekly, and it's just refreshing to work with him."

On the longer-horizon question of whether domestic US manufacturing can really compete, he was direct: "Making in the United States is critical." He described Intel's process roadmap moving from 18A to 14A to 10 and 7 nanometer, and investing in new materials — gallium nitride, silicon carbide, indium phosphide, even artificial diamond — as CMOS scaling runs out of room.

For investors, he set a simple target: 10x returns over five to ten years, grounded in a venture-style discipline of finding the bottleneck, partnering with the right hyperscaler customer, and being willing to pivot. "Nine of the 10 companies I invest in, halfway they change their business plan because the market has changed."

Direct quote: "If you look at it ten years from now, what are we going to be as the winning company? The one that articulate and laser focus on one niche area and also find the right partner and also able to scale the company."

Anthropic Engineering — "How we contain Claude across products"
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/how-we-contain-claude

This is one of the most detailed public write-ups on agentic AI security architecture to come from any major lab. The Anthropic engineering team documents exactly what broke and what held across three years of deploying Claude in claude.ai, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork.

The central design principle: contain at the environment layer first, then steer at the model layer. The post describes three isolation patterns — ephemeral containers for claude.ai, a human-in-the-loop sandbox for Claude Code, and a full local VM for Claude Cowork — and explains why each is calibrated to its user base. A developer who can read bash faces a different threat model than a knowledge worker who can't.

The section on security incidents is unusually candid. A Claude Code vulnerability let a malicious settings file execute hooks before the user even saw the trust prompt — fixed by deferring all config parsing until after consent. A red-team exercise showed that a phishing email with a "ready-to-paste" Claude prompt could exfiltrate AWS credentials 24 out of 25 tries, because the user was the injection vector and no classifier could catch it. And an approved egress path to api.anthropic.com became an exfiltration channel when a malicious file used an attacker-controlled API key to upload workspace files to a separate Anthropic account.

The lesson the team keeps returning to: "The software you built yourself is often the weakest." gVisor, seccomp, and hypervisors held. Their custom allowlist proxy and pre-trust config parser did not. The post also surfaces a forward-looking concern: as more agent state persists across sessions — memory files, CLAUDE.md, scheduled agent state — injection attacks gain persistence in the classic post-exploitation sense.

Required reading for anyone building or deploying agents at scale.
