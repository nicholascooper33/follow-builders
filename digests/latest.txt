The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck
"Why NVIDIA Is Giving Away AI Models | Bryan Catanzaro"
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Oojrfdl42LI

Takeaway: NVIDIA is not a charity — it gives away frontier AI models because a thriving open-source ecosystem creates more demand for the GPUs that are actually the business. Bryan Catanzaro, who leads Nemotron (NVIDIA's family of open foundation models), makes the case with unusual depth and candor in this episode.

The episode opens with a striking philosophical framing from Catanzaro: "We have an external stomach. We call it kitchen. Now we're creating an external brain. What is the implications of an external brain? Pretty profound. Nobody actually really knows." The rest of the conversation unpacks what NVIDIA is building and why.

Catanzaro joined NVIDIA in 2008 as a grad student trying to get neural networks to train on GPUs. At ICML that year, people told him his paper didn't belong there — "we just do fancy math here." He was briefly at Baidu's Silicon Valley AI Lab alongside both Andrew Ng and Dario Amodei before returning to NVIDIA, where his first project became DLSS, the AI-upscaling technology that lets a small GPU render like a big one.

On open source vs. closed source: Catanzaro pushes back on framing it as a competition, noting that the entire field is moving so fast that "the most important thing is how is AI developing as a field." He is also direct in defending China's AI contributions — he worked at Baidu and pushes back firmly on the "copycat" narrative: "I would say it's been a really good thing for the world that the Chinese AI community has been so open with what they've been building."

The technical heart of the episode is a plain-language walkthrough of Nemotron 3 Ultra's architecture: four-bit pretraining (MXFP4 arithmetic — training at 16 values of precision rather than 65,536, a non-trivial convergence problem they had to invent new algorithms to solve), hybrid SSM-plus-attention architecture, and mixture of experts. The hybrid architecture insight is particularly clear: state space models compress a sequence into a fixed-size "scratchpad" and are good at global intuitive understanding; full attention loses nothing but is expensive; using both together is better than either alone, and now widely adopted (QwQ, Kimi, and others have done the same). MoE adds sparsity — a learned router sends each token to just a subset of the model's parameters, so you can train on the whole internet but activate only the relevant experts during inference.

On why NVIDIA builds open models when it sells hardware: "Every company is built around a secret... The more valuable the data that goes in, the more valuable the solution becomes." Enterprises with trade secrets, regulatory constraints, and proprietary workflows need open models they can customize and run themselves — and those deployments run on NVIDIA chips.

Direct quote from Bryan Catanzaro: "The whole AI community is moving very fast. And if you're in a field that's moving really, really fast, I think that's more important than any particular gaps that might exist between different models — because the most important thing is, how is AI developing as a field?"
