The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck — "OpenAI Board Member Zico Kolter on the Real Risks of Frontier AI"
Published: May 7, 2026
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DvyZcCfepeI

Takeaway: Safety does not scale automatically with model capability — it requires explicit, ongoing investment — and the attack surface for AI agents is fundamentally different and more dangerous than for chatbots.

This is a rare and substantive conversation with Zico Kolter, head of the Machine Learning Department at Carnegie Mellon University and chair of OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee (SSC). Matt Turck of FirstMark Capital interviews Kolter across a wide range of topics: how OpenAI's safety governance actually works in practice, why bigger models do not automatically become safer, what modern jailbreaks look like, and how agentic systems have transformed the threat landscape.

Kolter explains that the SSC's role is analogous to a corporate audit committee — it reviews models before release, receives third-party safety reports, and can delay launches when questions remain unresolved. He is candid that this process happens for every major model release, with ongoing dialogue between the committee and researchers so there are rarely surprises.

On safety and scale, Kolter makes a key distinction: across nearly every capability domain, waiting for a bigger model means getting a better one. But robustness to manipulation does not work that way. He co-founded Grace One, a startup that runs large-scale red-teaming competitions and automated adversarial evaluations for major AI labs, including the largest agentic red-teaming competition in history — 1.8 million attack attempts.

A highlight of the episode is his four-category taxonomy of AI risk: (1) model mistakes and hallucinations, (2) harmful use by bad actors, (3) societal and psychological effects, and (4) loss of control. He argues that AI safety must address all four — ignoring any one creates blind spots.

On agents specifically, Kolter introduces prompt injection as the defining new attack vector: malicious instructions embedded in third-party data — emails, web pages, tool responses — that an agent processes and then acts on as if they were legitimate user commands. He advises builders to think carefully about what permissions and credentials agents hold, because the blast radius of a compromised agent scales directly with its access level.

He is bullish on agents in production today — with proper sandboxing and limited permissions — and offers a surprising optimistic take on mechanistic interpretability: coding agents, he argues, may finally make MechInterp into a real science by automating the exploratory research work at scale.

The episode ends with a discussion of his free course at somemodernAIcourse.org, where he builds a complete LLM from scratch — including RL and tool calls — in approximately 200 to 300 lines of Python. "AI systems are incredibly simple," he says. "The entire complexity of an AI system evolves from the data they're trained on."

Direct quote: "We can delay model release if we feel that we need to understand that better. So far, we have not seen that same thing happen when it comes to things like the robustness of models. You can't just sort of trust models to get safer by getting bigger."
