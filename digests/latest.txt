Aaron Levie — CEO at Box
Aaron made the most-shared point of the day: CEOs are uniquely prone to what he calls "AI psychosis" because they interact only with AI's happy path and never face the downstream work that actually makes agents useful. Playing with AI, they generate a prototype or a contract and feel they've "got it" — but they didn't have to review the code before it shipped, or verify every legal term before a counterparty saw it. His prescription: use AI so heavily that you develop a real appreciation for both the upside and the genuine effort involved in getting sustainable results from agents.

Nikunj Kothari — Partner at FPV Ventures
Nikunj shared a detailed practical technique for automating tasks on websites that can't be reliably navigated via the DOM: use Claude Code with browser_use or vanilla Playwright to sniff network requests while you click around on the sections you care about. Claude Code then reads the logs, figures out the API structure, authentication (usually cookie-based), and rate limits through trial and error — giving you a clean programmatic interface to any website. He extrapolates that every website will soon need to be headless, and we'll need mechanisms for agents to pay websites programmatically, plus a tools.txt equivalent (analogous to llms.txt) so agents can discover what tools are available.

Unsupervised Learning — Ep 86: Yann LeCun on Leaving Meta, Breaking The LLM Paradigm, & Why Hinton is Wrong
Published: 2026-05-15 | YouTube: https://www.youtube.com/@RedpointAI

One-sentence takeaway: Yann LeCun argues LLMs are genuinely useful but structurally incapable of reaching human-level intelligence, and he left Meta to pursue a fundamentally different architecture — JEPA-based world models — through his new company AMI (Advanced Machine Intelligence) Labs.

LeCun, one of the Turing Award co-winners and the founder of Meta's AI Research lab (FAIR), sat down with the Unsupervised Learning podcast to explain his technical thesis and the circumstances that led him out of Meta. His core claim is clear and carefully stated: "There's nothing wrong with LLMs in the sense of LLMs… They're great for what they do. They're just not a path towards human level or human-like intelligence or even animal-like intelligence." His reason: LLMs predict the next token; they have no ability to predict the consequences of their own actions and no ability to plan by search or optimization. Both capabilities, he argues, are essential to intelligent behavior.

His alternative is the JEPA architecture — Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture — which LeCun has been developing at FAIR for years and is now scaling at AMI Labs. Rather than training a model to reconstruct or generate pixels (which he calls "a losing proposition"), JEPA learns abstract representations by predicting the latent encoding of one corrupted view from another. His key insight, supported by years of empirical results: "All of the architectures that have been successful to learn representations of images and videos are non-generative architectures. And all the generative ones basically have been failures." Dino, iJEPA, and VJEPA are the lineage he points to.

On world models specifically, LeCun offers a crisp definition: "A world model, at a very general level, is something that allows an agentic system to anticipate the consequences of its own actions." Without that, he says, any planning an agent does is structurally unsound — you're optimizing a sequence of tokens, not searching for a good sequence of actions. His diagnosis of VLAs (vision-language-action models used in robotics) is blunt: "pretty much now seen as a failure" — too brittle, too data-hungry, no generalization to new tasks because there's no internal model to reason from.

On Meta: LeCun recounts that after Llama 4 disappointed Mark Zuckerberg, Meta reorganized Gen AI and refocused the entire company on catching up in the LLM race. Exploratory research — including his world model work — was sidelined. He had backing from Zuckerberg and CTO Andrew Bosworth for the world model direction, but most applications he saw for JEPA were in industrial settings (manufacturing, etc.) that Meta had no interest in. When results started getting good and it became clear the project needed to transition from research to product development, spinning out was the natural move.

Direct quote — on what good research leadership looks like:
"The best way to get breakthrough research is you hire the best people, and you get the f*** out of the way."
