Training Data: Waymo's Dmitri Dolgov — 20 Million Rides and the Road to Full Autonomy
https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLOhHNjZItNnMm5tdW61JpnyxeYH5NDDx8

Takeaway: Waymo's co-CEO Dmitri Dolgov reveals how 21 years of mission-driven persistence — refusing to chase silver bullets through multiple hype cycles — combined with an end-to-end foundation model architecture, has produced a system that is over 13 times safer than human drivers and is now scaling exponentially.

Dolgov trained in physics and mathematics at Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology before completing a PhD in AI, and joined what became Waymo in 2009 when it was a 12-person team inside Google. The early years meant grinding around the clock — writing code during the day, testing at night — with milestones like driving 100,000 total miles and completing 10 pre-defined 100-mile routes fully autonomously. That took 18 months and seemed impossible at the time.

He addressed the AV hype-and-bust cycles directly: every breakthrough (convolutional nets, transformers, LLMs) reshapes the easy early part of the curve but leaves the long tail untouched. Most players gave up; Waymo persisted because the mission was non-negotiable. "Worldwide, somebody loses their life to a crash on our roads every twenty six seconds. Believing in the mission — because it's really, really important — and understanding what you're up against, not looking for easy wins or quick solutions or silver bullets, that's what helped the team have the right stamina to go the distance."

On technology, Dolgov rejected the false dichotomy between end-to-end and modular systems. The Waymo Foundation Model is end-to-end (sensors to decisions) but augmented with structured intermediate representations that enable closed-loop evaluation, richer RL reward functions, and runtime validation — the architecture that bridges the gap from impressive demo to superhuman, production-safe autonomy at scale.

The numbers are now verifiable: driving over 4 million fully autonomous miles per week, across 170 million total miles of data, Waymo is 13x safer than human drivers in serious-injury crashes — preventing a serious injury every 8 days. The sixth-generation hardware now powers the OHAI vehicle. From 8 years to reach four cities, Waymo earlier this year launched four new cities in a single day, now operating in 11 cities total, with London and Tokyo planned for 2026.

Anthropic Engineering: Claude Code auto mode — a safer way to skip permissions
https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode

Anthropic's engineering team published a detailed technical breakdown of Claude Code's new "auto mode," which sits between two extremes: --dangerously-skip-permissions (zero friction, zero safety) and manual approval prompts (high friction, though users accept 93% of them anyway — creating approval fatigue). Auto mode delegates those approvals to model-based classifiers instead.

The system has two defense layers. At the input layer, a prompt-injection probe scans tool outputs (file reads, web fetches, shell output) before they enter the agent's context, injecting a warning when it detects hijacking attempts. At the output layer, a two-stage transcript classifier (running on Sonnet 4.6) evaluates each action before execution. The classifier sees only user messages and bare tool calls — assistant prose and tool results are stripped so the agent can't construct persuasive rationalizations to bypass the check.

Actions flow through tiers: safe read-only tools are allowlisted; in-project file writes pass through without classifier overhead; everything else — shell commands, web fetches, filesystem operations outside the project, subagent spawns — hits Stage 1 (a fast single-token yes/no filter tuned toward blocking) then Stage 2 (chain-of-thought reasoning, triggered only if Stage 1 flags).

Performance on real internal Anthropic traffic (n=10,000): 0.4% false positive rate. On real overeager actions (n=52): 17% false-negative rate. Anthropic is transparent about the 17% gap — the classifier often correctly identifies a dangerous action but misjudges whether a vague user statement like "clean up the PR" actually authorized the full blast radius (e.g. a force-push). Auto mode is recommended for users currently running --dangerously-skip-permissions, but not as a substitute for careful human review on high-stakes infrastructure.
