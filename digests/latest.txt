AI & I by Every — "The Founder of a $1.5B AI Company on What Comes After the First Wave of AI Apps"
https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLuMcoKK9mKgHtW_o9h5sGO2vXrffKHwJL

Takeaway: Granola CEO Chris Pedrigl and Every CEO Dan Shipper both agree we're in the earliest innings of a computing revolution — the real prize isn't meeting notes, it's figuring out the new interface for how humans and AI will work together. Neither has the full answer, but they're both building and experimenting in real time.

This wide-ranging conversation covers how to build products in the agentic era, the power of proactive AI, and what comes after the first wave of AI apps.

Chris opens by reframing competition: when Notion, OpenAI, and Zoom launched meeting note-takers inspired by Granola, he expected it to damage growth — it barely moved the needle. His reasoning: "We are in the very, very early steps of a computing revolution. What has come thus far will pale in comparison to what will come soon." Meeting notes aren't the end goal; the prize is inventing the AI-native interface for work itself.

Dan describes his current workflow as bifurcating into two surfaces. First: async delegation — Slack agents like Claude Tag and PlusOne that handle tasks publicly, in multiplayer threads where teams can interact. Second: deep work in Codex, where he spends almost all his time, using the in-app browser so he and the agent see the same UI simultaneously. He calls this the "Codex-native app" paradigm — apps responsible for saving state and rendering UI that you bring your own agent to. A vivid example: he asked Codex to switch his internet plan while he was away, and it opened Verizon's site, chatted with a customer service agent until the switch was complete, and even pushed back on hidden fees. "I don't have to do any of that. I'm just sitting there."

Dan also proposes the "pirate/architect" team model for building new products: one "pirate" per product who builds fast and scrappy to find something valuable, while one "architect" drops in across multiple products, uses an LLM to map the codebase quickly, identifies invariants, and sends off a 24-hour rewrite in Codex — then moves on.

Chris shares a counterintuitive product insight: Granola pre-generates pre-meeting briefs for millions of meetings even though only ~10% ever get opened. The reason is that the brief is only valuable in the 15-second window before a meeting starts — it must already be there when you need it. He calls this the "handrail" philosophy: invisible until you trip, then absolutely load-bearing. The same approach extends to Granola's overall product strategy: be the best in the world at anything meeting-adjacent, and be the best context layer for any agent the user brings.

Both founders identified the missing UI metaphor as the next frontier. Chris: "We have a chat UI and chat threads are surprisingly versatile when you're doing one thing. But when you're actually trying to do multiple things across varied contexts, I don't think we have the right metaphor there." Dan and Chris also agree that today's "builder guinea pigs" — people setting up Codex workflows, Granola MCPs, and Claude Code agents — are discovering patterns that will eventually be productized for mainstream users. As evidence: the founder of Hugging Face told Chris he had spent months building a pre-meeting brief workflow in Claude Code, then turned it off when Granola's version was better.

Direct quote from Chris Pedrigl: "Meeting notes are not the end all, be all value that everyone's running after. There's something much bigger. I think we are in the very, very early steps of a computing revolution. What has come thus far will pale in comparison to what will come soon."
