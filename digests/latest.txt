Madhu Guru — Former Product Lead at Google for Gemini, Veo, and Nano Banana

Madhu mapped out a three-phase progression he observed in enterprise AI adoption. Phase 1 (2024): everyone defaulted to GPT for everything, because it was the shiny new thing. Phase 2 (early 2025): teams overcorrected by hunting for the smallest, cheapest model without having evals sophisticated enough to match tasks to models — burning cycles and shipping slower. Phase 3 (now): the most sophisticated AI-native startups decompose their products into sub-agents and route each task to the right model — hardest reasoning to Claude, simplest to Gemini Flash-Lite or open-weight models. Enterprises typically trail AI-native builders by 6–9 months.

Unsupervised Learning — Ep 89: AI Research Legend's Honest Assessment of Where We Are
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N1geOimmdDo

One-sentence takeaway: Lucas Kaiser, co-author of the original transformer paper, gives a rare insider view that current AI is genuinely astonishing — but that something fundamental about generalization remains unsolved, and honest researchers don't fully understand what tipped coding AI from impressive to indispensable.

Lucas Kaiser — a co-author of "Attention Is All You Need" and veteran of both Google and OpenAI — joins Unsupervised Learning for a wide-ranging conversation on where AI actually stands. Kaiser opens with the generalization question that has dominated AI discourse. Transformers with reasoning, agents, and tool access can do "amazing things" he says he wouldn't have believed possible two years ago. But they still need staggering amounts of data to learn what humans grasp intuitively. As he puts it:

"LLMs, they will learn the concept. They will learn it. But after exhausting all other options. You need this trillion tokens... You need to learn all the surface level things, and only when that doesn't explain something, they will finally learn the concept. That's not how we learn."

He draws a sharp analogy to Waymo: millions of miles of simulation and real driving, and yet the system still can't reliably handle a highway construction zone the way any teenager can. It points to a form of generalization that current architectures haven't cracked.

On why Anthropic led in coding, Kaiser offers a striking observation: Anthropic made a deliberate strategic bet on coding in part because they felt they couldn't compete with OpenAI on general chat. That focus paid off. The broader coding breakthrough around last Christmas, Kaiser says, wasn't attributable to any single change — it was a combination of better base models, post-training tweaks, improved agent harnesses, and compaction techniques. He's candid that even insiders don't fully know what specifically pushed coding AI from "useful" to "indispensable."

The episode also covers why current RL approaches are unlikely to produce researcher-level AI yet (rollouts would take weeks, making training take months), the open-versus-closed-source trajectory, and whether a post-transformer architecture is ultimately needed. Kaiser remains genuinely open about the uncertainty — he sees credible arguments on both sides — and finds that uncertainty itself the most exciting part of the research frontier right now.
